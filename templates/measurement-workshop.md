# The Measurement Workshop

*Estimate, measure, and prove it — with rulers, scales and timers*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 7–11 · **Chinese name:** 测量工坊 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on measurement course for curious kids: every lesson starts with a brave estimate, then proves it with a real tool — a ruler, a scale, or a timer. Learners measure length, weight, and time, record results in a clear table, work out the difference between their guess and the real number, and learn when centimeters, grams, or seconds are the right unit to use. No worksheets for their own sake: every number comes from measuring something real in the room.

## Features

- Estimate first, then prove it with a real tool
- Length, mass, and time — with the right unit every time
- Tables, labelled drawings, and charts kids fill in themselves
- Difference and perimeter worked from real measurements
- Parent summary: estimates getting closer over time

## How it works

1. Estimate first — write a brave guess before any tool comes out
2. Prove it with a ruler, a scale, or a timer, and record the real number in the table
3. Work out the difference, choose the right unit, and watch the estimates get closer

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · The Desk That Was Longer Than It Looked (complete worked lesson)

THE DESK THAT WAS LONGER THAN IT LOOKED — a complete lesson, exactly as the learner meets it.

STEP 1 — ESTIMATE FIRST (no tools yet). Alex looks at the desk and writes a brave guess: 'I think the long edge of my desk is 110 centimetres.' A guess is not a mistake — it is a number to beat.

STEP 2 — MEASURE IT. Alex lays a 30 cm ruler along the long edge, end to end, marking each end with a fingertip so nothing is counted twice. The ruler fits exactly 4 times. Working: 4 × 30 cm = 120 cm. The desk is 120 cm long.

STEP 3 — HOW CLOSE WAS THE GUESS? Difference = 120 cm − 110 cm = 10 cm. Sentence for the table: 'My estimate was 10 cm too short — the desk was longer than it looked.'

STEP 4 — SAME DESK, OTHER UNIT. 120 cm is about 47 inches, because 120 ÷ 2.54 = 47.2, which rounds to about 47 inches. Rule to remember: use centimetres for a desk, metres for a room, and kilometres for a trip — pick the unit that keeps the number friendly.

STEP 5 — A SECOND OBJECT, START TO FINISH. A picture book. Estimate: 30 cm tall. Measure the height: 24 cm. Measure the width: 17 cm. Difference for the height: 30 cm − 24 cm = 6 cm too tall. Perimeter — all the way around the cover: 24 + 17 + 24 + 17 = 82 cm. Check by grouping: (24 + 17) × 2 = 41 × 2 = 82 cm. Both ways agree: 82 cm.

STEP 6 — TIME COUNTS TOO. Fill a measuring cup to 250 ml while a timer runs. Alex estimates 20 seconds. The timer says 15 seconds. Difference: 20 − 15 = 5 seconds too long. Sentence: 'Pouring is faster than it feels.'

THE RECORDING TABLE (filled in during the lesson): Object | What we measured | Estimate | Real measure | Difference — Desk, long edge, 110 cm, 120 cm, 10 cm. Book, height, 30 cm, 24 cm, 6 cm. Book, cover perimeter, no estimate, 82 cm, —. Water, time to pour 250 ml, 20 seconds, 15 seconds, 5 seconds.

CLOSE — ONE HONEST SENTENCE. Alex writes: 'My eyes guess short for long things and long for fast things, so I will estimate, then always prove it.' Next lesson: kitchen scale — estimate a lemon, then weigh it in grams.
