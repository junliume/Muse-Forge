# The Story Quilt

*Stitch a story together, one patch at a time*

**Category:** Storytelling · **Ages:** Ages 4–9 · **Chinese name:** 故事拼布被 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A cosy, take-turns storytelling studio where a story is built the way a quilt is made — one patch at a time. The listener lays out six patch cards in any order they like, and each patch opens one short, complete scene: a sun patch always begins warmly, a rain patch always brings a small problem worth solving kindly, a kitchen patch always smells of something baking. Because the listener chooses the order, the same six patches make a different story every time, and the storyteller pauses after the third and fifth patches so the listener can predict, add one detail, or choose what the character notices next. Every session ends by "sleeping under the finished quilt" — a one-sentence retelling by the listener, in their own words, kept in the parent log. No coins, no timers, no scores, no rankings and no right-or-wrong answers: just slow, warm stories about kindness, patience and noticing small things, stitched at the pace the child sets.

- Six patch cards, any order — the listener's order makes a new story every time

1. Lay out the six patch cards face up and let the listener choose the order, one patch at a time
2. Tell each patch as one short scene, pausing after patch three and patch five for the listener to join in
3. Finish with the rest patch, then a one-sentence retelling as the quilt is 'folded away' for the night

## Sample content

### Sample · First Quilting: Alex and the Centre Patch (complete story in six patches)

THE FIRST QUILTING — Alex and the Centre Patch

HOW THIS SESSION WORKS: Six patch cards lie face up on the table — Sun, Garden, Rain, Kitchen, River and Star. Alex chooses the order, one patch at a time, and the storyteller tells one short scene for each patch. After the third patch and after the fifth patch, the storyteller stops and Alex joins in. Alex chose this order: Sun, Garden, Rain, Kitchen, River, Star. This is the story that order made.

PATCH ONE — THE SUN PATCH (every sun patch begins warmly). On the first morning of the holidays, Alex woke to find that the quilt at the end of the bed had a hole in its very middle — not a tear, but a neat square space where the centre patch ought to be. Folded on the pillow was a note in round, friendly handwriting: 'A quilt is not finished until its centre patch has a story in it. Go and collect one. — Weaver Wren.' Alex put on slippers, tucked the note into a pocket, and stepped out into a garden full of early sun.

PATCH TWO — THE GARDEN PATCH (every garden patch is for noticing small things). The garden was doing its quiet morning work. A snail was crossing the path with its whole house on its back, in no hurry at all. 'Good morning,' said Alex, and stepped carefully around it, because small travellers deserve the whole path. By the bean rows, Alex noticed something that had not been there yesterday: a single sunflower, no taller than a spoon, had opened overnight and turned its face to the sun. Alex crouched down. 'You first,' Alex whispered. 'Tell the sun I said thank you for warming my quilt.' The sunflower, being a sunflower, said nothing — but it stood a little straighter, the way listeners do.

PATCH THREE — THE RAIN PATCH (every rain patch brings a small trouble worth solving kindly). Then, quite suddenly for a sunny morning, a cloud the size of a laundry basket stopped over the garden and began, politely but thoroughly, to rain — only on the garden, and only for a little while. The snail did not mind. The sunflower did not mind. But the note in Alex's pocket did mind: the ink began to run and blur. Alex cupped both hands over the pocket and ran, not to the house, but to the old pear tree, the garden's one dry place, and waited there, hands over pocket, while the small rain drummed on the leaves.

[PAUSE — the storyteller stops here. Ask: 'What do you think Alex will find under the pear tree?' Let the listener predict. Every answer is welcome; the story continues with what Alex noticed next, and the listener's idea is woven in by name in the telling.]

PATCH FOUR — THE KITCHEN PATCH (every kitchen patch smells of something baking). Under the pear tree stood a basket that Weaver Wren had left for the gardeners: three warm rolls, a small jar of plum jam, and — folded underneath, dry as a bone — a square of plain white cloth, exactly the size of the hole in the quilt. Alex understood at once: the centre patch was not lost. It was blank on purpose. It was waiting. Alex ate one roll slowly, because discoveries are better with jam, and saved the other two, because a story might have company later.

PATCH FIVE — THE RIVER PATCH (every river patch is for sharing the journey home). At the bottom of the garden, the little river was running high and busy after helping the cloud. Alex could not step across with the cloth, the rolls and dry feet all at once — until the snail, who had finished crossing the path, looked at the stepping stones and then at Alex, as if to say: slowly counts too. So Alex went the slow way, stone by stone, one careful step at a time, holding the white patch high and dry — and did not wobble once, because there was no hurry, and hurry was never the point.

[PAUSE — the storyteller stops here. Ask: 'We have one patch left, the Star patch, and it is not night yet. What should Alex do while waiting for the first star?' Let the listener choose: in this telling, Alex shared the last two rolls on the doorstep and told the whole day back, start to finish.]

PATCH SIX — THE STAR PATCH (every star patch ends in rest). When the first star came out, small and steady, Alex laid the white patch in the middle of the quilt — and Weaver Wren, who had been waiting with needle and rose-red thread, stitched while Alex told the story: the sun that warmed, the garden that noticed, the rain that tested, the kitchen that provided, the river that taught slowness. With every part of the telling, the plain white patch seemed less plain, though nobody could say exactly why. 'There,' said Weaver Wren, tying the last knot. 'A patch with a story in it will keep anybody warm.' Alex slept under the finished quilt that night, in the very middle of the story.

THE RETELLING (the listener's turn, in their own words — one sentence is plenty): For example: 'Alex found a blank patch and filled it with a whole day.' The parent log keeps the listener's sentence exactly as spoken.

STITCH IT AGAIN: Next session, lay the same six patches in a different order — start with Rain, or save the Sun for last — and notice how the same day becomes a brand-new story. The quilt remembers every order it has ever been told in.
