# The Attic Telescope Tales

*A new star each night, a gentle tale to match*

**Category:** Storytelling · **Ages:** Ages 5–9 · **Chinese name:** 阁楼望远镜故事 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A calm bedtime storytelling app built around one small ritual: climb to the attic window, point the little brass telescope at one star, and hear the gentle tale that star has been keeping. Each night the listener chooses a star by its colour, its twinkle, or the tiny picture it seems to draw in the sky — never by speed or score — and the tale unfolds in three short parts, pausing after each part for the listener to predict, add one small detail, or choose what the character notices next. The cast is small and kind: an owl librarian who catalogues the stars, a firefly lamplighter who lights the attic lamp, a grandmother astronomer, and a neighbourhood cat who mostly naps. Every session closes with the chosen ending ritual — a one-sentence star wish, retelling the brightest moment, or drawing tomorrow's star — kept in the parent log. No coins, no timers, no rankings, no right-or-wrong answers: just slow, warm stories about wonder, patience and noticing small bright things, told at the pace the child sets.

## Sample content

### Sample · First Looking: Alex and the Star That Blinked Back (complete story)

FIRST LOOKING — Alex and the Star That Blinked Back

The attic stairs creaked in their friendly places, one creak for the bottom step and one for the top, and Alex climbed them slowly with the blanket over one arm. At the round window stood the little brass telescope, no taller than a stool, with its three wooden legs planted on the rug like a patient dog. Hoot the owl librarian was already there, perched on the bookshelf with the star catalogue open on her knees. She catalogued every star the telescope had ever found, in handwriting so neat it looked printed.

'Good evening, Alex,' said Hoot. 'The lamp, if you please, Lumen.' Lumen the firefly lamplighter drifted down from the rafters and lit the attic lamp with a soft green-gold glow, then settled on the telescope's rim to warm his wings. 'One star tonight,' said Hoot, turning the catalogue's pages. 'Choose it the kind way — by its colour, by its twinkle, or by the picture it draws. Never by hurry.'

Alex pressed one eye to the telescope and the sky came close, the way a whisper comes close. There were silver stars and honey stars and one very small rose-coloured star at the edge of everything, twinkling slowly, as if it were in no rush at all. 'That one,' said Alex. 'The rose one. It looks like it is waiting for somebody.'

Hoot smiled the way owls smile, mostly with their eyes. 'Then listen,' she said. 'That star has been keeping a story.'

PART ONE — THE LIGHTHOUSE AT THE EDGE OF THE SEA. Once, on the last point of land before the deep water, there stood a small lighthouse with a rose-coloured lamp, and its keeper was a girl named Wren — no relation to any other Wren you may have met — who was exactly as tall as the telescope is now. Every night Wren climbed the lighthouse stairs, one creak for the bottom step and one for the top, and lit the rose lamp so the small slow boats could find the harbour. The big fast ships had their own bright lights and did not need her. But the small slow boats — the mussel boat, the letter boat, the boat that carried grandmothers and soup — those boats steered by Wren's rose light, and they trusted it completely.

[PAUSE — PREDICT] Here Hoot stopped and folded her wings. 'Alex, what do you think happened on the night of our story?' Alex thought about the rose lamp and the small slow boats. 'I think the lamp went out,' said Alex, 'and Wren did not know what to do.' Hoot nodded slowly. 'Hold that thought,' she said. 'The star remembers what really happened.'

PART TWO — THE NIGHT THE LAMP WENT OUT. Alex was right — or nearly. On the night of our story the wind came up out of the deep water, worrying at the lighthouse door, and the rose lamp flickered once, twice, and went dark. Wren stood in the windy dark at the top of the stairs and her heart knocked like a spoon in a cup. Out on the water she could hear the small slow boats ringing their little bells, polite and worried. Wren very nearly ran down the stairs to shout for help. But then she remembered what the old keepers teach: you cannot hurry a lamp, and you cannot shout a light back on. So Wren did the hardest trick a keeper knows. She stayed. She wrapped her hands around the cool glass to keep the wind from it. She told the dark, out loud, exactly where the harbour was, the way you would talk a friend home. And she waited — one slow breath, then another — while below her, on the rocks, the fireflies of that coast rose up out of the grass, one by one, green and gold, and arranged themselves along the harbour path like a sentence written in light.

[PAUSE — ADD A DETAIL] 'Your turn to give the story something,' said Hoot. 'One small detail — anything you like.' Alex looked at the lamp on the rug and added: 'There was one very small firefly who was late, and he hurried, and the others saved him a place in the line.' 'Then he is in the story now,' said Hoot, 'and the star will keep him safe.'

PART THREE — WHAT THE ROSE LIGHT SAW. At last the wind spent itself against the door and went away to worry some other coast. Wren's lamp, rested and ready, bloomed back into rose light all on its own — the way lamps in stories do when somebody has been patient with them. And the small slow boats came in one by one: the mussel boat, the letter boat, and the boat that carried grandmothers and soup, and every captain waved up at the rose window, and Wren waved back with both arms. Far out at the edge of the water, where the harbour path ended, the fireflies lifted off the rocks in a slow green-gold swirl and wrote themselves back into grass. And the very smallest firefly, the late one, blinked twice at the lighthouse before he went — blink, blink — the way a friend says goodnight without words. Wren blinked back. Twice.

Back in the attic, the rose star at the edge of the sky twinkled slowly in its telescope frame. 'It blinked,' whispered Alex. 'It blinked back.' 'Stars keep the stories of everyone who was ever patient under them,' said Hoot, closing the catalogue. Lumen turned the attic lamp down to its sleepiest glow. And Alex chose tonight's ending the kind way: not the drawing, not the wish — the retelling. 'The brightest moment,' said Alex, 'was when the late firefly blinked, and Wren blinked back.' Hoot wrote it in the catalogue in her printed-looking handwriting, under the rose star, where it will keep until the next clear night.
