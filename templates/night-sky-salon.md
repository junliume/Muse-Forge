# The Night Sky Salon

*An unhurried evening of moon, planets and good conversation*

**Category:** Grown-ups · **Ages:** Adults · **Chinese name:** 夜空沙龙 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A slow-evening salon for grown-ups who want to look up again. Each session gives you one clear sky focus — the Moon, a bright planet, or a constellation with its story — a simple way to find it from a balcony, garden or park, and conversation prompts that turn looking into talking. No expertise and no telescope are required: the guide teaches the naked-eye skills first (letting your eyes adapt, using your fist to measure angles in the sky, telling a steady planet from a twinkling star), and only then offers an optional closer look. Every evening ends with a one-page sky journal — a sketch, three things you actually saw, and one question to carry to next time. Unhurried by design: no streaks, no scores, no homework.

## Sample content

### Sample · Evening One: The Crescent Moon and the Evening Star (a complete salon)

EVENING CARD — Host: Alex · Pair · Gentle wonder · About 30 min · Balcony & window · One-page sky journal. Tonight's focus: the crescent Moon and the evening star. No telescope needed — eyes, patience and a warm drink are the whole kit.

1 · SETTLE (5 minutes)
Alex dims the lights and says: "Welcome to the Night Sky Salon. Tonight we look up — slowly. Two things only: a young crescent Moon, and the brightest light in the twilight. Phones face-down now; the sky gets our whole attention." Pour something warm. Then let your eyes adapt: full night vision takes about 20 minutes to arrive, and it comes in stages — colours fade first, then faint stars begin to appear one by one, as if someone were turning the sky up very slowly. Every glance at a bright screen resets the clock, so the phones stay down. Sit. Sip. Let the dark do its work.

2 · FIND THE MOON
Face west, where the Sun went down. On a crescent evening the Moon hangs there in the fading light — a slim, bright curve, lit on its sunward side: after sunset that is the western side, the side facing the sunset, so the crescent tips toward where the Sun set. Look closely at the dark part of the disc: it is not quite black. A faint grey glow fills it in — that is earthshine, sunlight reflected off the Earth onto the Moon's night side, sometimes called 'the old Moon in the new Moon's arms.' The Moon runs through all its phases in about 29.5 days, so come back at the same time tomorrow and the crescent will have grown a little fatter, and climbed a little further from the sunset. SKETCH IT: in the journal circle, draw the crescent's lit edge as one clean curve, and write beside it which side is lit — the sunward, western side tonight.

3 · FIND THE EVENING STAR
Now find the brightest "star" in the western twilight. That is no star at all — it is Venus, the brightest planet in our sky. Venus is never seen far from the Sun: it never strays more than about 47 degrees from it, so it belongs to twilight — low in the west after sunset, or low in the east before sunrise, and never overhead at midnight. THE STEADY-LIGHT TEST: watch Venus, then watch a true star. The planet shines steadily; the star twinkles. Starlight reaches us as a near-perfect point, and moving air jiggles that point around so it flickers — but a planet, though tiny, is a small disc, and its light averages out across the disc, so it holds calm and steady. THE FIST MEASURE: hold your fist out at arm's length — it covers about 10 degrees of sky. Venus at its farthest from the Sun sits about four to five fists from the sunset point. Measure it tonight and see.

4 · ONE STORY
Long ago, sky-watchers who rose before dawn greeted a brilliant Morning Star, and the same watchers, at dusk, saluted a brilliant Evening Star — and for ages people treated them as two different lights, with two different names and two different stories. The Evening Star was the herald of rest; the Morning Star, the herald of work. It took patient watchers many evenings of notes to accept the gentle truth: the two were one world all along, swinging round the Sun on the inside of our own path, now catching the light on one side of it, now on the other. Two names, one neighbour. Alex says: "Every clear evening, Venus still plays both parts — we just know now it is the same friend arriving."

5 · TALK
Take one card each, or pass them around. Short answers are welcome; a pass is always a complete answer.
(1) "What is your earliest memory of the night sky — where were you, and who showed it to you?"
(2) "What do you only ever notice when you slow right down — in the sky, or anywhere?"
(3) "If a visitor from far away stood here for one evening, what one thing about our home sky would you most want to show them?"

6 · CLOSE WITH THE JOURNAL
One page, no more. At the top, a big circle: inside it, tonight's sky sketch — the crescent with its earthshine ghost, Venus steady nearby. Under the circle, three lines, one for each thing you actually saw — not hoped to see, saw: "1. The crescent's lit edge, sharp as a nail paring. 2. The dark part of the Moon, faintly grey. 3. Venus holding steady while the star beside it twinkled." Then one line for tonight's question, the one you carry to next time: "How much fatter will the crescent be in three evenings?" Alex reads the closing line aloud: "We looked up, we slowed down, and the sky kept its side of the conversation. Goodnight — same time, next clear evening."
