# The Decimal Point Workshop

*Zoom into the number line — tenths, hundredths, and numbers that live between*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 8–11 · **Chinese name:** 小数点工坊 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on decimal workshop built on one big idea: a decimal is not a new kind of number, it is a whole number that has been zoomed in on. Learners start with a metre ribbon cut into 10 equal tenths, then zoom again into hundredths on a hundred-square, and only then meet the decimal point as the marker that says where the wholes stop and the pieces begin. Comparing decimals is taught the honest way — by lining up place values, never by "longer looks bigger" — and adding decimals is done on a place-value chart so tenths land under tenths. Every lesson moves from ribbon or square, to number line, to symbols, and ends with a self-check the learner can run alone. Nothing is timed.

## Features

- Zoom-in number line: 0 to 1 stretches into tenths, then one tenth stretches into hundredths
- Place-value chart on every problem: ones, tenths, hundredths in fixed columns so digits line up honestly
- The longer-is-not-bigger lesson: 0.65 beats 0.7? No — compare tenths first, and the chart proves it
- Ribbon and hundred-square models first; symbols only after the picture makes sense
- Adding and subtracting decimals by columns, with a "line up the points" rule the learner can say aloud
- Money as one example among several — lengths, weights and litres carry equal weight, so decimals are not "just prices"
- No timers, no speed scores — a decimal is understood when the learner can place it, compare it, and explain the zoom

## At a glance

- One metre ribbon, cut into 10 equal pieces: each piece is 0.1 of the ribbon — the first zoom
- 0.7 and 0.65: the tenths decide — 7 tenths beats 6 tenths, so 0.7 is bigger even though 65 looks longer
- Add 0.4 + 0.35 on the chart: tenths under tenths, hundredths under hundredths — 0.75, every digit in its column

## How it works

1. Build the decimal on the ribbon, hundred-square, or number line first
2. Place each digit on the place-value chart and say its value out loud
3. Write the decimal sentence that matches the picture and the chart
4. Check it yourself: re-place the answer on the zoomed number line and confirm it lands where it should

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · The Ribbon Zoom: Tenths, the Longer-Is-Not-Bigger Trap, and Adding 0.4 + 0.35 (complete worked lesson)

WELCOME TO THE WORKSHOP. In this workshop a decimal is never a strange new number — it is a familiar number seen through a magnifying glass. Today we zoom in twice, escape the most common decimal trap, and add two decimals without a single digit landing in the wrong column.

THE FIRST ZOOM. Take a ribbon exactly 1 metre long. Cut it into 10 equal pieces. Each piece is one tenth of the ribbon, and we write it 0.1. Three pieces: 0.3. Seven pieces: 0.7. The digit after the point counts tenths — nothing more mysterious than that. On the number line from 0 to 1, the tenths stand like fence posts: 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, all the way to 0.9, and then the whole ribbon, 1.0.

THE SECOND ZOOM. Now take just one tenth — one small piece — and cut that into 10 equal slivers. Each sliver is one hundredth of the whole ribbon: 0.01. On the hundred-square, the whole square is 1, each row is a tenth, and each small cell is a hundredth. Sixty-five cells shaded is 0.65: 6 tenths and 5 hundredths, and the place-value chart shows exactly that — ones column 0, tenths column 6, hundredths column 5.

THE TRAP. Which is bigger, 0.7 or 0.65? The trap whispers: 65 is bigger than 7, so 0.65 must win. The chart gives the honest answer. Compare the tenths first, because tenths are the bigger pieces: 0.7 has 7 tenths; 0.65 has only 6 tenths. Seven tenths beats six tenths before the hundredths are even invited to speak. So 0.7 is bigger. Rule worth saying aloud: compare column by column, starting with the biggest pieces — longer is not bigger.

ADD ON THE CHART. Add 0.4 + 0.35. Write them on the chart with the points lined up in a straight column: 0.4 is 0 tenths? No — 0.4 is 4 tenths and 0 hundredths; 0.35 is 3 tenths and 5 hundredths. Add the hundredths: 0 + 5 = 5 hundredths. Add the tenths: 4 + 3 = 7 tenths. Answer: 0.75. Check on the number line: 0.4, then hop 0.3 to 0.7, then hop 0.05 to 0.75. The line and the chart agree, so the answer is finished.

YOUR TURN (answers in brackets): 1) A ribbon shows 0.6 shaded. How many tenths is that, and how many hundredths on the square? [6 tenths; 60 hundredths.] 2) Which is bigger, 0.45 or 0.5? Use the tenths column to prove it. [0.5 — 5 tenths beats 4 tenths.] 3) Place 0.35 and 0.53 on the zoomed number line. Which sits further right? [0.53.] 4) Add 0.2 + 0.47 on the chart. [0.67 — 2 + 4 tenths, 0 + 7 hundredths.] 5) A jug holds 0.8 of a litre. Is that more or less than 0.75 of a litre? [More — 8 tenths beats 7 tenths.] 6) Sam writes 0.9 = 0.09. Use the chart to explain the mistake. [0.9 is 9 tenths; 0.09 is 0 tenths and 9 hundredths — the 9 sits in a smaller column.]

WORKSHOP PROMISE: zoom it, chart it, compare the biggest pieces first, line up the points — then write it. A learner who can do all four owns decimals for good.
