# The Tea Tasting Journal

*A slow tasting notebook for leaves, steam and quiet notes*

**Category:** Grown-ups · **Ages:** Grown-ups · **Chinese name:** 品茶手记 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A calm tasting journal for grown-ups who enjoy comparing teas slowly. Each guided tasting session walks through four unhurried steps — dry-leaf aroma, first steep, second steep and finish — with a plain sensory vocabulary to borrow from (floral, grassy, roasted, citrus, honey, mineral), so noting what you actually taste never feels like an exam. Side-by-side comparison cards put two teas next to each other, note against note, and every session adds to a growing personal flavor map: the words you reach for most, the teas that surprised you, the ones you would happily brew again. There are no health claims here and no wellness promises — the journal is purely sensory, cultural and social: taste, notice, write it down, share a cup. However you take it — a solo quiet cup at the kitchen table or a small group passing the pot — the ritual is the same: pour slowly, pay attention, and let the page keep what the palate found.

## Sample content

### Sample Session · Jasmine Green, Four Steeps of Noticing (complete guided session)

TASTING CARD — Host: Alex · Solo quiet cup · About 20 min · Green teas · Classic tasting words · Short phrases. Tonight's tea: a jasmine green, the kind scented with fresh jasmine blossoms, chosen because the tin has been sitting unopened since spring and quiet evenings are for opening things slowly.

SET THE TABLE (2 minutes)
Warm the cup first: pour a little hot water into the tasting cup, swirl it once, and pour it away — a warm cup keeps the first steep honest. Put one level teaspoon of leaves in the small pot; the leaves are long, twisted and deep green, with pale jasmine petals folded in among them. Boil the kettle, then let it sit off the boil for a full minute — green tea likes water that has just stopped shouting. Open the journal to a fresh page and write the date, the tea's name, and nothing else yet. The first step belongs to the nose, not the pen.

STEP 1 — DRY LEAF (smell before water)
Tip a pinch of the dry leaves into your warm palm and breathe over them. First impression, in short phrases from the word bank: floral — strongly floral, jasmine first, like walking past a night garden. Underneath it: grassy, a fresh green smell, the way a lawn smells after it is cut. And at the very back, something sweet and round — honey, faint but really there. Write exactly three phrases in the journal: "jasmine, loud and lovely", "cut grass", "a whisper of honey". If you smell something the word bank doesn't name, write your own word beside the bank's — the bank is a starter, not a fence. Alex's own extra word tonight: "porch" — because the dry leaf smells like sitting on a porch in June, and that is a true and useful note.

STEP 2 — FIRST STEEP (water just off the boil, 2 minutes)
Pour the water over the leaves in a slow circle and start the timer: two minutes, no more. Watch while you wait — the leaves sink, then rise, then sink again, opening as they go; tasters call this the agony of the leaves, but it looks more like a small, slow dance. Pour the whole steep into the warm cup so the taste is even. COLOUR: pale gold-green, clear as glass, bright at the edges. AROMA: the jasmine is softer now, and warmer — less garden, more perfume held at arm's length; the grassy note has become steamed greens, gentle and cooked. TASTE (small sip, let it sit on the tongue, breathe out through the nose): floral first, then grassy-sweet, then a clean mineral line at the sides of the tongue, like spring water over smooth stones. No bitterness at two minutes — the water temperature did its job. Journal lines: "gold-green, clear", "jasmine, softer warm", "sweet grass, then mineral", "no bitter edge".

STEP 3 — SECOND STEEP (2 minutes again, same leaves)
Refill the pot with water just off the boil and steep two minutes once more. This is the step tasters wait for, because the second steep is where a tea shows what it is made of. COLOUR: a shade deeper, still bright. AROMA: the jasmine has stepped back to a supporting role; now the leaf itself leads — grassy, warm, a little like sweet hay. TASTE: rounder and sweeter than the first steep; the honey note that hid in the dry leaf has arrived properly now, in the middle of the sip. The floral is a frame, not the picture. The finish is shorter than the first steep's but cleaner. Journal lines: "deeper gold", "jasmine steps back", "honey arrives", "rounder, sweeter, cleaner end". Write one honest sentence about the change: "Steep one was the blossom; steep two is the leaf — and I like the leaf better."

STEP 4 — FINISH AND AFTERTASTE
Take one last slow sip and pay attention to what stays. The aftertaste is floral again, returning like a song you can still hear from another room, with the mineral line underneath it, cool and steady. It lasts about half a minute before it fades to plain sweetness. Nothing harsh arrives late; nothing asks to be rinsed away. A good finish, the journal says, is one you notice without trying.

VERDICT AND BREW-AGAIN RATING
One-line verdict: "A garden first, then a meadow — jasmine up front, honey underneath, and a finish like spring water." Brew-again rating: 5 out of 5 — brew again soon, and next time try the second steep at two and a half minutes to see if the honey deepens. Filed under: brew-again favorites, first page of the green-tea section of the flavor map — tonight the map gained floral, grassy, honey and mineral, all in one small cup.

SIDE BY SIDE — TONIGHT'S CUP AGAINST A ROASTED OOLONG
Last week's roasted oolong, from its card: dark amber colour, roasted and toasty up front, dried-fruit sweetness, a long warm finish. Set beside tonight's jasmine green, the difference is a whole posture: the oolong leans back — roasted, deep, evening-heavy — while the jasmine green stands in a doorway with the light behind it: floral, grassy, bright. Neither wins. The map now holds two far-apart points, and the space between them is exactly where the next teas will land.
