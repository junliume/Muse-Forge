# The Museum of Small Hours

*Tiny nighttime exhibits that each tell one gentle story*

**Category:** Storytelling · **Ages:** Ages 4–8 · **Chinese name:** 小时光博物馆 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A gentle co-created storytelling template set in a tiny museum that only opens after supper. Each glass case holds one small, ordinary thing — a button, a kettle, a seed — and each thing holds one short story. Your child chooses a case, Muse tells one slow opening line, and together you build the exhibit's story one beat at a time: Muse always repeats your child's line back to confirm before offering the next line, so the child always leads the plot. A clock mouse, a moth curator and a teacup owl keep company and bring the jokes; they never decide what happens and never give clues. Every visit ends with the whole story read back as one smooth tale, a new label card written for the case in your child's own words, and a calm closing line as the museum lights dim.

## Sample content

### Sample · Night One: The Kettle That Remembered Rain (complete worked visit)

SESSION CARD — Storyteller: Alex · Ages 6–7 · English · About 20 min · Gallery: Everyday wonders · Crew: the whole crew · Pace: Gentle · Ending: Full read-back + calm close.

HOW THE MUSEUM WORKS
The Museum of Small Hours only opens after supper, when the street is quiet. Inside there is one long room, and along the walls stand glass cases, each holding one small, ordinary thing. Every thing in a case is waiting to tell the story of the one day it remembers best. You choose a case. Muse tells the first slow line of its story. Then it is your turn: you add one beat, Muse repeats your beat back exactly as you said it — so you can hear it is truly yours — and only then does the story take its next step. The clock mouse, who lives inside the big hall clock, taps the time gently and nothing more. The moth curator dusts the labels and takes her job very seriously. The teacup owl, who is exactly as tall as a teacup, whispers the name of each case as you pass it. They keep us company and make us smile. They never decide what happens. You do.

CHOOSING A CASE — EXACTLY THREE CASES TONIGHT
Muse: "Alex, tonight the museum has lit exactly three cases for you. In the first case: a round blue button, with four holes, from a sailor's coat. In the second case: a small copper kettle, a little dented, that still smells faintly of rain. In the third case: one striped seed, no bigger than your thumbnail, sleeping on a cushion of moss. The teacup owl is whispering their names as we pass — button… kettle… seed. Which case is yours tonight?"
Alex: "The kettle. The one that smells of rain."
Muse: "The kettle — the one that smells of rain. It is yours tonight. The moth curator is dusting its label twice, because a chosen case deserves a very clean label."

THE OPENING LINE
Muse: "On the last rainy evening of autumn, the little copper kettle sat on the stove in the quiet house, and listened — the way it always listened — for the rain to come back and tell it the end of a story the rain had started long ago."
Muse: "That is the kettle's first line, Alex. The kettle is listening for the rain. What happens next?"
BEAT ONE — Alex: "The rain comes back. But only a little bit. Four taps on the window."
Muse: "The rain comes back — but only a little bit. Four taps on the window. Yes, exactly as you said it. And listen with me, Alex: tap… tap… tap… tap. Four taps, no more. The kettle holds its breath — well, it holds its steam, which for a kettle is the same thing. The clock mouse taps four times inside his clock, very softly, because four is the number he heard, and a clock mouse always keeps the count honest."
BEAT TWO — Alex: "The kettle sings a tiny song to answer the rain."
Muse: "The kettle sings a tiny song to answer the rain — that is your beat, word for word. So the kettle lifts its lid, just a crack, and out comes the smallest song a kettle can sing: not a whistle, not yet — a hum, low and round, like a bee remembering summer. The rain taps softer, so it can hear. The moth curator has stopped dusting. Even curators stop for a song."
BEAT THREE — Alex: "Then the rain tells the kettle the end of the story. The story was about a garden that needed one more rain."
Muse: "Then the rain tells the kettle the end of the story — and the story was about a garden that needed one more rain. Exactly yours, Alex. So the rain finishes its story, right there at the window: long ago it had promised a small dry garden one more rain before winter, and tonight, with four taps and a kettle for a witness, the promise is kept. Somewhere past the quiet street, that garden is drinking its last long drink of the year. The kettle's hum settles. Its dent catches the lamplight. The story is complete."

THE READ-BACK (the whole visit, read as one smooth story)
"On the last rainy evening of autumn, the little copper kettle sat on the stove in the quiet house, listening for the rain to come back and tell it the end of a story. The rain came back — but only a little bit: four taps on the window. The kettle sang a tiny song to answer the rain. And the rain told the kettle the end of the story: it was about a garden that needed one more rain, and tonight the promise was kept."
THE LABEL CARD (pinned to the case, in Alex's own words): "The kettle that listened for the rain, and sang back."
CALM CLOSE — Muse: "The museum lights are dimming now, one case at a time. The kettle's label is pinned straight — the moth curator checked it twice. The clock mouse is closing his clock door. Goodnight, Museum of Small Hours. Goodnight, Alex. The cases will keep their stories safe until you choose the next one."
