# The Sharing Fair Workshop

*Share it fair, group it smart — division you can see, check, and trust*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 7–10 · **Chinese name:** 公平分享工坊 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on division workshop where every problem starts as something real to share or group — muffins on a tray, ribbons in bundles, chairs in rows — before it is ever written with a ÷ sign. Learners meet division in its two true shapes: sharing (split 12 among 3 friends) and grouping (how many groups of 3 hide inside 12), and discover both give the same answer. Leftovers are not mistakes: a remainder lesson teaches what to do with the 2 strawberries that cannot be split fairly. Every answer is checked backwards with multiplication, so the learner never has to take a result on faith, and nothing is timed.

## Features

- Two shapes of division, taught side by side: sharing out and grouping up — same numbers, same answer
- Remainders handled honestly: leftovers are named, drawn, and given a sensible fate (save, split smaller, or set aside)
- Check-it-backwards rule: every division answer is verified with its multiplication partner before moving on
- Fairness first: sharing problems always ask "does everyone get the same?" before "how many each?"
- Arrays reused from multiplication: 3 rows of 4 is 12, so 12 shared into 3 rows puts 4 in each
- Word problems set at a picnic, a craft table, and a market stall — never bare number drills alone
- No timers, no speed scores — a division fact is ready when the learner can share it, group it, and check it

## At a glance

- Share 12 muffins among 3 friends: 4 each — and 12 grouped into bags of 3 makes 4 bags. Same 12, same 4
- Leftovers are information: 14 berries shared by 4 is 3 each with 2 left over, and the 2 get drawn, not hidden
- Every answer checks backwards: 12 ÷ 3 = 4 because 3 × 4 = 12

## How it works

1. Act the problem out on the sharing tray or grouping mat with real counters
2. Say it both ways: shared among, and grouped into
3. Write the division sentence that matches the picture
4. Check it backwards with multiplication before the answer counts as finished

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · The Picnic Tray: Sharing 12, Grouping 12, and the Two Berries Left Over (complete worked lesson)

WELCOME TO THE SHARING FAIR. In this workshop, division is never a symbol to memorise — it is something fair that you do with your hands. Today we share a picnic, pack it into bags, and meet the most honest idea in division: the leftover.

SHARE IT OUT. Here is a tray of 12 muffins and 3 friends at the picnic blanket. Deal them out one at a time, like cards: one for you, one for you, one for you — and round again. Count a friend's pile: 4 muffins. Everyone's pile matches, so the sharing is fair. Written as numbers: 12 ÷ 3 = 4. The first number is the whole tray; the second is how many friends share it; the answer is how many each friend gets.

GROUP IT UP. Same 12 muffins, new job: pack them into bags of 3 for the walk home. Fill a bag — 3. Fill another — 6 counted. Another — 9. Another — 12. How many bags? 4 bags. Written down: 12 ÷ 3 = 4. Notice the trick the workshop loves: the numbers did not change, and neither did the answer. Sharing among 3 and grouping by 3 are two doors into the same room.

CHECK IT BACKWARDS. Never trust an answer you cannot check. The multiplication partner of 12 ÷ 3 = 4 is 3 × 4 = 12 — three piles of four muffins rebuild the whole tray. If the partner does not rebuild the tray, the division is not finished yet.

MEET THE LEFTOVER. Now 14 strawberries and 4 friends. Deal them out: 3 rounds use 12 berries, and then 2 berries sit on the tray. They cannot be dealt fairly — a fourth round would need 4. So we say it honestly: 14 ÷ 4 = 3 each, with 2 left over. The leftover is not a mistake; it is information. The workshop asks what a sensible person does with it: save the 2 for tomorrow, or cut each berry smaller and share the pieces. What we never do is pretend the 2 are not there. Check backwards: 4 × 3 = 12, plus the 2 left over makes 14 — the tray is rebuilt, leftovers and all.

YOUR TURN (answers in brackets): 1) Share 10 ribbons among 5 dancers. How many each? [2 each — check: 5 × 2 = 10.] 2) 18 chairs, grouped in rows of 6. How many rows? [3 rows — check: 3 × 6 = 18.] 3) Share 15 grapes among 4 friends. What happens? [3 each, with 3 left over — check: 4 × 3 = 12, plus 3 makes 15.] 4) A stall packs 24 apples into boxes of 4. How many boxes? [6 boxes.] 5) Is 20 ÷ 5 the same answer shared out or grouped up? Prove it both ways. [Yes — 4 either way: 4 in each of 5 piles, or 4 groups of 5.] 6) You share 11 pencils among 3 classmates and get 3 each with 2 left over. Check it backwards. [3 × 3 = 9, plus 2 left over makes 11 — correct.]

WORKSHOP PROMISE: share it, group it, name the leftover, check it backwards — then write it. A learner who can do all four owns division for good.
