# The Weather Window

*Look up, log the sky, and learn to predict tomorrow*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 5–9 · **Chinese name:** 天气之窗 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A daily sky-watching lab where a child looks out a real window (or at a photo of the sky), names the clouds, reads simple instruments, logs the day in a weather journal, and makes a friendly prediction for tomorrow that gets checked the next day. Sessions are short, hands-on, and screen-light: observe first, then log and learn one small weather idea at a time (cloud types, wind clues, rain measurement, temperature feel, seasonal change). A parent view shows the week's journal and which predictions came true — celebrated for noticing, never scored for being right.

## Features

- Observe first: 2 minutes of real sky-watching before any screen
- One weather idea per session: clouds, wind, rain, temperature, seasons
- A picture weather journal: draw the sky, log it in words
- Predict tomorrow, check it next day — noticing is the win
- Parent week view: journal entries and prediction follow-ups

## Sample content

### Sample Session · Day 1: Naming the Clouds (complete worked session)

SESSION CARD — Observer: Alex · Ages 7–8 · English · About 15 min · Observation style: Real window first · Journal style: Draw + one sentence. The rule of the lab: look first, screens second — eyes on the real sky before anything is logged.

1 · LOOK (2 minutes at the window)
Stand at the window and just look for two whole minutes — longer than it sounds, and that is the point. Find three things before you say anything at all:
• One cloud that is moving, and one thing in the sky that is not moving at all.
• The brightest part of the sky, and the greyest part.
• One sound the weather is making — wind in a tree, rain on the sill, or the quiet of a still morning.
Now say the sky's sentence out loud, in one breath: 'Today the sky is…' and finish it with what you truly saw. Alex's sentence today: 'Today the sky is blue with white puffy clouds drifting slowly east, and the air feels cool on my cheeks.'

2 · NAME THE CLOUDS — TODAY'S MINI-LESSON
Clouds are made of tiny water droplets or ice crystals floating in the air, and sky-watchers sort them by shape and height. Today we learn three:
• CUMULUS — the puffy ones. Cumulus clouds look like heaps of cotton wool or cauliflower, with flat bottoms and rounded, billowing tops. That flat bottom is real: it is the level where rising air cools enough for its invisible water vapour to turn into visible droplets, which is why every cumulus cloud in the sky grows its base at about the same height. On their own, puffy cumulus with flat bottoms usually mean fair weather — a good day to be outside.
• STRATUS — the blanket. Stratus spreads out in a flat grey layer, like a sheet pulled across the whole sky, with no edges you can point to. It forms low down, and when it thickens it can bring drizzle — rain so fine it feels like mist on your face.
• CIRRUS — the feathers. Cirrus clouds are thin, wispy streaks very high up, so high and so cold that they are made of ice crystals instead of water droplets. They often look like hair being combed across the blue.
Today's sky has cumulus: three puffy clouds with flat bottoms, sailing slowly. Alex points to the biggest one and names it out loud: 'Cumulus — flat bottom, fair day.'

3 · WIND CLUES (rules of thumb, not measurements)
You cannot see wind — only what it moves. These are gentle rules of thumb for young observers, not exact measurements:
• Leaves fluttering on the tree = a light breeze is passing.
• Whole branches swaying = the wind is stronger now.
• A flag snapped straight out, tugging at its pole = strong wind.
Today the leaves on the street tree are fluttering, but the branches are still, and no flag is flying straight. Alex writes the clue in the journal: 'Wind: light breeze (leaves only).'

4 · LOG IT — TODAY'S JOURNAL PAGE
Draw the sky first, words second. Alex's worked entry for today:
THE DRAWING: a rectangle window. Inside it, three cumulus clouds with flat bottoms drawn in pencil and shaded lightly underneath; a small sun in the top corner; the street tree at the edge with its leaves scribbled mid-flutter.
THE LABEL: one cloud is labelled in careful letters — CUMULUS.
THE SENTENCE (one sentence about the air): 'The air felt cool and smelled like cut grass, and the clouds moved slower than the birds.'
That is a complete journal page: a drawing, a label, a wind clue and one true sentence.

5 · PREDICT TOMORROW — AND CHECK IT
A prediction is a careful guess built from what you observed — not a wish. Alex uses today's evidence: puffy cumulus clouds with flat bottoms, only a light breeze, and no grey blanket of stratus anywhere.
Alex's prediction, written at the bottom of the page: 'Puffy clouds with flat bottoms and only a light breeze — I predict tomorrow morning starts fair, and I'll check at breakfast.'
THE NEXT-DAY CHECK RITUAL: tomorrow, before anything else, go back to the same window at breakfast time. Look for two minutes. Was the morning fair? Then the journal gets a small sun stamp beside the prediction — not because being right is the prize, but because you noticed, predicted and checked. If the sky did something else entirely, that is even more interesting: write what it actually did under the prediction, and wonder why. Weather changes its mind; good observers just keep watching.

PARENT NOTE: The win in this lab is never 'the prediction came true.' It is the habit underneath — look first, name what you see, write it down, and check tomorrow. A week of journal pages shows the pattern better than any single day: which clouds visited, how the wind behaved, and how your young observer's predictions slowly sharpen.
