# The Multiplication Meadow Workshop

*Build times tables as rows and groups — never as chants to memorise cold*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 7–10 · **Chinese name:** 乘法草地工坊 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on multiplication workshop where learners build every fact as a visible array — rows of flowers, crates of apples, seats in a meadow theatre — before they ever say the answer out loud. Each lesson teaches one clear idea: equal groups, arrays, the turn-around fact (3 × 4 is 4 × 3 turned sideways), doubling and halving, and breaking a hard fact into two easy ones. Practice always starts from the picture, moves to the numbers, and ends with a check the learner can do themselves, so a fact is understood, built, and only then remembered. Difficulty follows the chosen fact families, hints rebuild the array rather than revealing the answer, and nothing is timed.

## Features

- Arrays first: every fact is built as rows and columns before it is written as numbers
- Turn-around facts shown by rotating the same array — the total never changes
- Break-it-apart strategy: 7 × 6 becomes 7 × 5 plus 7 × 1, with the pieces visible
- Doubling ladder: learn 2s, double to 4s, double again to 8s
- Skip-count paths through the meadow that land exactly on each multiple
- Mixed review that revisits older families spaced across sessions
- No timers, no speed scores — a fact is ready when the learner can build it and explain it

## At a glance

- One array, two facts: 4 rows of 3 flowers is 12 — turn it sideways and it is 3 rows of 4, still 12
- Break it apart: 7 × 6 = 7 × 5 + 7 × 1 = 35 + 7 = 42, with both pieces drawn
- The doubling ladder: 3 × 2 = 6, so 3 × 4 = 12, so 3 × 8 = 24

## How it works

1. Build the fact as an array of objects and count the rows and columns
2. Say and write the fact that matches the picture
3. Practise the same fact turned around, doubled, or broken apart
4. Check it yourself: rebuild the array a second way and confirm the total matches

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · The Flower Rows: Building 3 × 4, Turning It Around, and Breaking Apart 7 × 6 (complete worked lesson)

WELCOME TO THE MEADOW. In this workshop a times fact is never a sentence to memorise — it is something you build. Today we plant flower rows, turn them sideways, and learn the workshop's favourite trick for hard facts: break them apart.

BUILD IT FIRST. Plant 3 rows of flowers with 4 flowers in each row. Count the rows: 3. Count across one row: 4. Count them all by skip-counting the rows: 4, 8, 12. Three rows of four is 12 flowers. Written as numbers: 3 × 4 = 12. The first number counts the rows; the second counts how many sit in each row.

TURN IT AROUND. Now walk to the side of the same flower bed and look again. From here you see 4 rows of 3 flowers. Count: 3, 6, 9, 12. Still 12 — nobody planted or picked a single flower. Written down: 4 × 3 = 12. This is the turn-around rule: turning a fact around never changes its answer, so every fact you learn teaches you two facts at once.

DOUBLE IT. Plant 3 rows of 2: that is 6. Add the same planting again beside it and the rows become rows of 4: 3 × 4 = 12, exactly double. Double once more to rows of 8: 3 × 8 = 24. Doubling a known fact is a ladder — climb it instead of starting from the ground.

BREAK A HARD FACT APART. Now the hard one: 7 × 6. Do not guess. Split the 6 into 5 + 1, two pieces you already own. 7 × 5 = 35 — count the rows of five: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35. 7 × 1 = 7. Put the pieces together: 35 + 7 = 42. So 7 × 6 = 42, and you can prove it with the two smaller facts. Check by turning it around: 6 × 7 breaks into 6 × 5 + 6 × 2 = 30 + 12 = 42. Both roads arrive at 42.

YOUR TURN (answers in brackets): 1) Build 2 × 5 as rows and give the total. [2 rows of 5: 5, 10 — 10 in all.] 2) Turn around 5 × 3 = 15. What is 3 × 5? [Still 15 — the same array seen from the side.] 3) You know 4 × 2 = 8. Use doubling to find 4 × 4 and 4 × 8. [4 × 4 = 16; 4 × 8 = 32.] 4) Break apart 6 × 7 as 6 × 5 plus 6 × 2. [30 + 12 = 42.] 5) A stall packs 4 boxes with 6 apples in each box. How many apples? [4 × 6 = 24 apples.] 6) Is 8 × 3 the same as 3 × 8? Build or turn the array to prove it. [Yes — both are 24; turning the array around changes nothing.]

WORKSHOP PROMISE: build it, turn it, double it, break it apart — then say it. A learner who can build a fact owns it for good.
