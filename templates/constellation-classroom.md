# The Constellation Classroom

*Find Polaris, track the Moon, and read the night sky*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 6-10 · **Chinese name:** 星座小课堂 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on astronomy classroom for young sky-watchers: find north by the stars, follow the Moon through its whole cycle, and learn the seasonal constellations as stories you can actually point to. Every session mixes looking, drawing and measuring — a fist at arm's length is about 10 degrees, the two pointer stars of the Big Dipper lead straight to Polaris, and a simple nightly Moon sketch turns 29 nights into one clear pattern. No telescope needed; an open patch of sky, or the built-in indoor star maps on cloudy nights, is enough.

## Features

- Find Polaris in 3 steps using the Big Dipper pointer stars
- Moon-phase tracker: one sketch a night, a full 29.5-day cycle
- Seasonal sky guides: Orion in winter, Summer Triangle in summer
- Measure the sky with hands: a fist is about 10 degrees, a finger about 1 degree

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · Finding North: The Dipper, the Pointers, and Polaris (complete evening lesson)

EVENING LESSON — FINDING NORTH (about 20 minutes, open sky or balcony)

You need: tonight's sky, one hand, and the observation journal page for Lesson 1. No telescope. If it is cloudy, open Star Map 1 instead — every step below works on the map too.

WARM-UP — MEASURE WITH YOUR HAND (3 minutes). Hold your fist out at arm's length and close one eye. Your fist covers about 10 degrees of sky. One finger covers about 1 degree — about twice the width of the Moon, which is only half a degree across. A fully spread hand, thumb tip to little-finger tip, covers about 20 degrees. Sky distances are measured in degrees, and tonight your hand is the ruler.

STEP 1 — FIND THE BIG DIPPER (5 minutes). Face north and look up. Find seven bright stars shaped like a ladle, or a saucepan: four stars make the bowl, three stars bend away as the handle. The Big Dipper is not a constellation by itself — it is the brightest part of Ursa Major, the Great Bear. On spring evenings it sits high in the north; on autumn evenings it skims low near the horizon. Sketch the seven stars in your journal and join them with light lines.

STEP 2 — FOLLOW THE POINTER STARS (5 minutes). Look at the two stars that form the front edge of the bowl, the side farthest from the handle. The top one is Dubhe, the bottom one is Merak. Sky-watchers call them the Pointers, because the line through them points to Polaris. Dubhe and Merak are about 5 degrees apart — half a fist. Now hop that same gap five times in the same direction: 5 degrees, 10, 15, 20, 25 — about 28 degrees from Dubhe you land on a single, medium-bright star that sits almost alone. That is Polaris, the North Star. Check your count: five pointer-gaps, not four and not six. If you land on empty sky, start again from Dubhe and hop more slowly.

STEP 3 — CHECK THAT POLARIS EARNS ITS NAME (4 minutes). Polaris sits at the end of the handle of the Little Dipper, in Ursa Minor, the Little Bear. It looks ordinary — it is not the brightest star in the sky — but it sits almost exactly above Earth's North Pole, so as Earth turns, every other star appears to wheel around it while Polaris barely moves. That is why it matters: step outside, find Polaris, and you have found north, any night of the year. Drop an imaginary line straight down from Polaris to the horizon: that point is north. Turn 90 degrees to your right and you face east; behind you is south; to your left is west. Write N, E, S, W in your journal under your Dipper sketch.

MOON MINUTE (3 minutes). Before you go in, find the Moon if it is up and draw exactly the part that is lit. The Moon's shape changes because the Sun lights a different part of it as it orbits Earth, once every 29.5 days. The quarters are about 7 days apart: New Moon, about 7 days later First Quarter (the right half lit, for Northern Hemisphere watchers), about 7 days after that Full Moon, about 7 days later Last Quarter (the left half lit), then back to New. Tonight's drawing is Day 1 of your tracker — draw the Moon again tomorrow at the same time and compare: the lit part will have grown, and the Moon will sit a little farther east among the stars.

QUESTIONS — ANSWERS IN BRACKETS. 1) How far apart are Dubhe and Merak, in fists? [About half a fist — about 5 degrees.] 2) You hop the pointer gap 5 times from Dubhe. About how many degrees have you travelled? [About 25 to 28 degrees.] 3) Why does Polaris barely move while other stars wheel around it? [It sits almost exactly above Earth's North Pole, on the axis Earth spins around.] 4) A Full Moon was on Monday the 3rd. About what date will the Last Quarter Moon be? [About Monday the 10th — Full Moon to Last Quarter is about one quarter of the 29.5-day cycle, about 7 days.] 5) Your fist covers about 10 degrees. The Moon covers half a degree. About how many Moons fit across your fist? [About 20 — 10 divided by 0.5.]

KEEPER'S NOTE FOR GROWN-UPS. Everything in this lesson can be checked tonight: the pointer hop is the same in every season, only the Dipper's height changes. If city lights hide the fainter stars, Polaris and the Dipper's seven stars are usually still visible — they are among the brightest in the northern sky. Next lesson: Orion's belt in winter, and why Betelgeuse looks red while Rigel looks blue-white.
