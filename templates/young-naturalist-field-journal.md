# Young Naturalist Field Journal

*Observe, sketch and wonder — one habitat at a time*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 6–10 · **Chinese name:** 少年自然观察手记 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A field-journal template that turns any backyard, park corner or schoolyard tree into a real science investigation. Each session follows the same calm rhythm — sit, look closely, sketch what you actually see, count and compare, then ask a wonder question you cannot answer yet. No collecting live creatures, no made-up facts: the journal teaches the difference between 'I observed' and 'I think', labels drawings honestly, and builds a season-by-season record a young naturalist can look back on. Parent view shows completed entries, habitats visited and wonder questions still open.

## Features

- Sit-spot routine: sit, look, sketch, count, wonder — same calm rhythm every session

## Sample content

### Sample · Session One: The Maple at the Corner — Who Lives in One Tree? (complete session)

SESSION CARD — Naturalist: Alex · Ages 7–8 · English · About 20 min · Habitat: Trees & leaves · Season: Autumn · Style: Draw + write · Wonder style: Gentle wonder prompts.

1 · THE SIT-SPOT (about 4 minutes)
Choose one maple tree — the one at the corner of the park is perfect — and sit on the grass where you can see the whole tree, from the roots to the top branches. You do not need to touch anything yet. A sit-spot is just sitting still in one place and letting the place show itself to you.
Sit quietly for two whole minutes. That feels long. It is supposed to. Listen first: What do you hear? Leaves rubbing together when the breeze moves them. A bird calling from somewhere you cannot see. Far away, a dog, a gate, the street. Now look slowly, bottom to top: roots, trunk, low branches, high branches, sky behind the leaves. In your journal, write the date, the place, and one sentence: 'My sit-spot tree is a maple at the corner of the park.' That is the first honest line of your field journal — it says exactly where you were and nothing you did not see.

2 · BARK & TRUNK LOOK (about 5 minutes)
Walk up to the trunk and look closely — with your eyes, not by pulling anything off. The bark is grey-brown, and it is not smooth: it has long cracks running up and down, like the tree grew taller and the bark split into long strips to keep up. Some strips curl a tiny bit at the edges. Down low, near the roots, the bark is rougher and the cracks are deeper.
Now walk slowly around the whole trunk. On the side that faces the sun, the bark is dry and pale. On the side that stays in shadow, there is a soft green patch of moss, growing only there, hugging the bark in the damp shade. Only there. Remember that — it becomes a wonder question later.
SKETCH PROMPT: Draw the trunk as a tall rectangle, taller than it is wide. Draw the long cracks as wavy lines going up. Colour the moss patch green on one side only. Then label your drawing with these words and draw a thin line from each word to the thing it names: BARK · CRACK · MOSS · SHADOW SIDE / SUNNY SIDE. A labelled sketch is a scientist's drawing — it tells the truth about what you saw.

3 · LEAVES UP CLOSE (about 5 minutes)
Ground rule, and it matters: we only pick up leaves that have already fallen. A leaf still on the tree is the tree's food-maker, and it stays. A fallen leaf is finished and free to study — and afterwards we put it back where we found it.
Pick up one fallen leaf and hold it flat on your palm. A maple leaf has 5 lobes — five big points spreading out like the fingers of a hand, each ending in a pointy tip. Its stem is long and thin, almost as long as the leaf itself. Turn it over: the back is paler than the front, and you can see the veins like tiny roads. Lay the leaf on your journal page and trace around it carefully with your pencil. Then count the main veins — the thick ones that start where the stem meets the leaf and run out to the lobes. This leaf has 5 main veins, one for each lobe. Write that under your tracing: '5 lobes, 5 main veins, long stem.'
THE COMPARISON TABLE — same tree, two fallen leaves:
Fallen Leaf A — colour: green-yellow · size: 9 cm across · where found: in the sun, near the path.
Fallen Leaf B — colour: red-orange · size: 7 cm across · where found: in the shade, under the bench.
Both leaves fell from the same tree, and both are maple leaves with 5 lobes — but they are different colours and different sizes. Now the honest note, and every naturalist writes it: colour alone does not prove WHY they are different. Maybe one leaf got more sun. Maybe one is older. We do not know yet, and 'we do not know yet' is a proper science sentence. Write both leaves into the table, and leave the 'why' column empty for now.

4 · WHO VISITS? — A FIVE-MINUTE TALLY (about 5 minutes)
Stay by the trunk and watch for five minutes. Every visitor goes in the tally — but only what you truly see or hear, marked honestly. Here is today's tally, worked all the way through:
• Ants on the trunk: 3 small ants, one behind another, all walking up the same long crack in the bark. (Seen.)
• Spider web: 1 web stretched between two low twigs, shining when the light caught it. Spider seen? No — web seen, spider not seen, so the journal says: '1 web, spider not seen.'
• Birds: 2 heard calling from the high branches — but not seen. The journal records them exactly like this: '2 birds — heard, not seen.'
That last line is the heart of this whole journal. 'Heard, not seen' is not a failure. It is a careful observer telling the truth about how they know.

5 · I OBSERVED / I WONDER — THE CLOSING ENTRY
Every session ends the same way, in two columns.
I OBSERVED (things I saw with my own eyes):
1. The bark has long cracks running up and down, deeper near the roots.
2. Moss grows on the shaded side of the trunk only.
3. Three ants walked up the same crack, one behind another.
4. Two fallen leaves from the same tree were different colours: green-yellow (9 cm) and red-orange (7 cm).
I WONDER (questions I cannot answer yet — they stay open for next time):
1. Why is moss only on one side of the tree?
2. Do the ants follow the same crack every day?
Those two questions go on the parent wonder-question list, still open. Next session, same tree, we check the moss and the ant crack again — that is how a season-by-season journal grows.

PARENT NOTE: Nothing alive was collected, caught or moved in this session. The spider's web was looked at, not touched; the ants were counted, not disturbed; the two leaves studied were already fallen, and both were put back on the ground where they were found. The journal's one rule holds for every session: look, sketch, and leave the habitat exactly as you found it.
