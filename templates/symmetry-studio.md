# The Symmetry Studio

*Fold, flip and finish the other half*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 5–9 · **Chinese name:** 对称工作室 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on geometry studio where a child learns symmetry by doing it: fold paper to find the mirror line, finish the missing half of a picture square by square, and test which shapes look the same after a half-turn or a quarter-turn. Every activity follows the same honest rhythm — Predict, Fold or Draw, Check with the Mirror, Explain — with grid coordinates worked out in full so a child can verify every answer themselves. No timers, no scores for speed: the reward is the moment both halves match exactly.

## Features

- Fold-first learning — find the mirror line with real paper before drawing
- Grid-by-grid completion — every missing half worked out with coordinates
- Mirror-check in every activity — predict, then prove it
- Turns as well as flips — half-turn and quarter-turn symmetry tested by hand
- Parent guide with worked answers kept separate

- Fold the paper first — if the halves match edge to edge, you found the mirror line
- Finish the other half square by square — column 2 mirrors to column 7, row stays the same
- Half-turn test: does the shape look the same upside down? Check it, don't guess it

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · The Butterfly That Needed Its Other Wing (complete worked lesson)

THE BUTTERFLY THAT NEEDED ITS OTHER WING — A COMPLETE WORKED LESSON

Welcome to the Symmetry Studio, Alex. Today we finish a butterfly — but we do not guess a single spot. Every spot on the missing wing will be worked out, folded and checked, so that by the end you can prove the butterfly is right. Our rhythm is the studio rhythm, the same in every lesson: Predict, Fold or Draw, Check with the Mirror, Explain.

FIRST, THE ONE BIG IDEA. A line of symmetry is a fold line where the two halves match exactly, edge to edge and spot to spot. Here is the fold test, and it never lies: if every point on one half lands exactly on a point on the other half, the line is a line of symmetry. If even one point lands on empty space, it is not a line of symmetry — no matter how close it looks. Looking close is not enough in this studio. Folding is how we know.

OUR BOARD — THE 8-COLUMN MIRROR GRID. Today's butterfly lives on a grid with 8 columns and 6 rows. The fold line runs vertically between column 4 and column 5 — right down the butterfly's body. The mirror rule is simple, and the row never changes: column 1 mirrors to column 8, column 2 to column 7, column 3 to column 6, column 4 to column 5, and the row never changes. Say it once more, because it does all the work today: the column flips across the fold, and the row never changes.

STEP 1 — PREDICT. The left wing is already drawn, and it has three spots: Row 2 Column 2, Row 3 Column 3, and Row 4 Column 2. Before touching a pencil, Alex predicts where the matching spots on the right wing will be. 'The same rows,' Alex says, 'but flipped across the fold line.' A good prediction — now we prove it, spot by spot.

STEP 2 — FOLD OR DRAW. Work each spot with the mirror rule, counting steps from the fold. Spot one: Row 2 Column 2. Column 2 is 3 steps left of the fold, so its mirror is 3 steps right of the fold: Column 7. The row never changes, so the mirror spot is Row 2 Column 7. Spot two: Row 3 Column 3. Column 3 is 2 steps left of the fold, so its mirror is 2 steps right of the fold: Column 6 — Row 3 Column 6. Spot three: Row 4 Column 2. Column 2 is 3 steps left of the fold, so its mirror is 3 steps right of the fold: Column 7 — Row 4 Column 7. Alex draws the three new spots on the right wing: Row 2 Column 7, Row 3 Column 6, Row 4 Column 7.

STEP 3 — CHECK WITH THE MIRROR. Now the proof. Alex folds a paper copy of the grid along the fold line, between column 4 and column 5, and holds it up to the light. Every left-wing spot lands exactly on a right-wing spot — spot to spot, nothing left over and nothing missing. Then the counting check: count the spots on the left wing — 3 spots. Count the spots on the right wing — 3 spots. Three and three: the wings match, and the butterfly is whole.

A SECOND SHAPE — THE KITE. Same grid, same rule. A kite outline has three points on its left edge: Row 1 Column 4, Row 3 Column 2, and Row 5 Column 4. Column 4 sits right beside the fold, so it mirrors to its neighbour across the line: Column 5. Column 2 is 3 steps left of the fold, so it mirrors to Column 7. Column 4 at the bottom mirrors to Column 5 again. So the right edge of the kite is Row 1 Column 5, Row 3 Column 7, Row 5 Column 5 — and the rows never changed. Fold the paper and check: the kite's two edges land exactly on each other, point for point.

TURNS AS WELL AS FLIPS. Symmetry is not only about folding. Some shapes also look the same after a turn. Here is the hand test: trace the shape, turn the paper, check whether it fits its own outline. A square looks the same after a quarter-turn and has rotational symmetry of order 4 — it fits its own outline four times in one full turn. A rectangle that is not a square looks the same after a half-turn only, order 2. An equilateral triangle has order 3. And a circle matches at any turn — turn it a tiny bit or a lot, and it always fits. Alex tries the hand test with a paper square and counts out loud: quarter-turn, fits; half-turn, fits; three-quarter turn, fits; full turn, fits. Order 4, proven by hand.

STEP 4 — EXPLAIN. The last step belongs to Alex, in Alex's own words: 'Symmetry means the two halves are the same in mirror places. To find the other half, I count how far each spot is from the fold line, then I go the same distance on the other side, and I keep the row the same. Folding checks it, because if it is really symmetry, everything lands exactly.' That is the whole lesson in one breath — predict, work it out, fold to check, and say why.

NEXT TIME IN THE STUDIO: a paper snowflake with not one fold line but four — and a design-your-own-badge challenge, where Alex chooses the fold line first and builds both halves to match. Bring the mirror card.
