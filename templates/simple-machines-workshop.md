# Simple Machines Workshop

*Hands-on levers, pulleys and ramps kids build and test*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 7–11 · **Chinese name:** 简单机械工坊 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on workshop where kids discover how levers, pulleys, inclined planes, wheels, screws and wedges make hard jobs easier. Each session picks one machine, builds it from safe household-style materials in the app's guided steps, predicts what will happen, tests it, and explains the result in plain words. No dangerous tools, no real-world risk — all experiments are simulated step-by-step with clear cause and effect.

## Features

- 6 simple machines explored one at a time
- Predict -> Build -> Test -> Explain cycle in every session
- Worked measurements kids can check (distances, effort, counts)
- A workshop journal that records predictions vs results
- Gentle challenges that change one variable at a time

## What's included

- Machine library (6 machines)
- Guided build steps
- Prediction journal
- Parent progress view

## At a glance

- Predict first — guess whether the long or short lever lifts the heavy crate, then test it
- Build step by step — fulcrum, beam and load placed in order, one change at a time
- Journal proof — prediction vs result side by side, explained in the child's own words

## How it works

1. Pick a machine — choose one of the six simple machines to explore today.
2. Predict — write down what you think will happen before you build anything.
3. Build and test — follow the guided steps, changing one variable at a time.
4. Explain in the journal — compare prediction vs result in your own words.

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · The Lever Lift Challenge (complete worked lesson)

THE SETUP — On the workbench: a long sturdy beam (the lever), a triangular block (the fulcrum — the pivot the beam balances on), and a crate so heavy that Sam cannot lift it straight up, not even a little. The question of the day: can a machine lift what arms cannot?
PREDICT FIRST — Before touching anything, Sam writes a prediction in the workshop journal: “If the fulcrum sits right in the middle of the beam, pushing down on my end will lift the crate — but will it feel easy or hard?”
STEP 1 · FULCRUM IN THE MIDDLE — Sam slides one end of the beam under the crate, rests the beam on the fulcrum at its middle, and pushes down on the far end. The crate rises! But the push feels almost as hard as lifting the crate directly. Why? The beam is split into two equal arms: the effort arm (fulcrum to Sam's hands) and the load arm (fulcrum to the crate) are the same length, so the lever changes the direction of the push but gives no extra force.
STEP 2 · FULCRUM CLOSER TO THE CRATE — Now Sam moves the fulcrum much closer to the crate. The load arm is now just 1 unit long, and the effort arm is 3 units long. Sam pushes again — and the crate lifts with only about one third of the effort! Here is the verified explanation: when the load arm is shorter than the effort arm, the lever multiplies force. An effort arm of 3 units against a load arm of 1 unit means roughly one third the effort lifts the same crate. The trade: Sam's end of the beam travels about three times as far down as the crate rises. A lever trades distance for force — you push less hard, but you push farther.
STEP 3 · CHECK QUESTION — What happens if the fulcrum moves closer to where Sam pushes instead? Worked answer: it gets harder, not easier. Sliding the fulcrum toward the pushing end makes the effort arm shorter and the load arm longer, so the lever now multiplies force the wrong way — Sam would need even more effort than lifting the crate directly.
JOURNAL PROMPT — Finish this sentence in your journal: “A lever helps most when the fulcrum is close to the ______ because ______.” Then draw the beam, the fulcrum and the crate from Step 2, and label the effort arm (3 units) and the load arm (1 unit).
