# Fraction Kitchen Studio

*Learn fractions by measuring, mixing and sharing*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 6–9 · **Chinese name:** 分数小厨房 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on fractions studio set in a friendly pretend kitchen. Learners measure with cups and spoons, share pizzas and pies fairly, and follow short recipe cards — so a half, a quarter and a third are things you can see, pour and share, not just symbols on a page. Each session mixes a warm-up, one new idea taught step by step with kitchen pictures described in words, guided practice, and a short sharing challenge. Difficulty grows only when the previous idea is steady.

## Features

- Kitchen measures: halves, quarters, thirds, sixths and eighths you can picture
- Fair-sharing challenges: split pizzas, pies and pancake stacks evenly
- Step-by-step teaching: every new fraction is built from equal parts first
- Recipe-card sessions: warm-up, new idea, guided practice, sharing challenge
- Parent view: which fraction ideas are steady and which need another round

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · Recipe Card 3 — Halves, Quarters and Thirds (complete worked lesson)

RECIPE CARD 3 — HALVES, QUARTERS AND THIRDS
Warm-up (say the answers out loud; answers in brackets): If you cut a pancake exactly in the middle, how many equal pieces do you get? [2] What is one of those pieces called? [One half, written 1/2] If you cut a small pizza into 4 equal slices and eat 1 slice, what fraction did you eat? [1/4]

NEW IDEA — A fraction names EQUAL parts of one whole. The bottom number (denominator) tells how many equal parts the whole was cut into. The top number (numerator) tells how many of those parts we are talking about.
Kitchen picture 1 — Measuring flour: A recipe needs 1/2 cup of flour. Fill the half-cup measure to the top once. That is one part out of 2 equal parts that would make a whole cup. So 1/2 cup is less than a whole cup, and two half-cups make exactly 1 whole cup: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1.
Kitchen picture 2 — Sharing a pie: A berry pie is cut into 4 equal slices. Each slice is 1/4 of the pie. If three friends each take one slice, together they take 3/4 of the pie, and 1/4 is left. Check: 3/4 + 1/4 = 4/4 = 1 whole pie.
Kitchen picture 3 — Thirds with ribbon carrots: A carrot cake is cut into 3 equal pieces. Each piece is 1/3. Thirds are bigger than quarters, because the same whole is cut into fewer pieces: 1/3 > 1/4. Remember: the bigger the bottom number, the smaller each equal piece.

GUIDED PRACTICE (work each one, then check):
1) A recipe uses 1/4 cup of cocoa and then another 1/4 cup. How much cocoa in total? [1/4 + 1/4 = 2/4, which is the same amount as 1/2 cup]
2) A pizza is cut into 6 equal slices. You serve 5 slices. What fraction was served, and what fraction is left? [5/6 served, 1/6 left; 5/6 + 1/6 = 1 whole]
3) Which is more: 1/2 of a pancake or 1/3 of the same pancake? [1/2 is more — halves are bigger pieces than thirds]
4) A ribbon is cut into 8 equal strips. A bow uses 3 strips. What fraction of the ribbon is used? [3/8]

SHARING CHALLENGE — The Banana Bread Problem: A loaf of banana bread is cut into 8 equal slices. Four friends want to share the loaf fairly and eat the whole loaf. How many slices does each friend get, and what fraction of the loaf is that? Work it: 8 slices ÷ 4 friends = 2 slices each. Each friend gets 2/8 of the loaf, and 2/8 is the same amount as 1/4. Follow-up: if a fifth friend arrives before anyone eats, can the 8 slices still be shared exactly fairly with nothing left over? [No — 8 cannot be split into 5 equal whole-slice shares; you would need to cut slices smaller, for example cut each slice in half to make 16 half-slices, then each friend gets 3 half-slices with 1 half-slice left, so a truly exact fair share needs a different cut — a good discussion question, not a trick.]

Parent note: All foods are pretend recipe cards. No real cooking, heat or allergens are involved; the kitchen is a picture-and-story setting for making equal parts visible.
