# The Riverstone Story Circle

*Stories told one smooth stone at a time*

**Category:** Storytelling · **Ages:** Ages 4–9 · **Chinese name:** 河石故事圈 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A calm, take-turns storytelling circle where each smooth river stone a child picks up opens one short, complete tale. The listener chooses a stone by colour, size or pattern — never by speed or score — and the storyteller unfolds a gentle story in three short parts, pausing after each part for the listener to predict, add one detail, or choose what the character notices next. Every session ends with the stone being 'returned to the river' — a one-sentence retelling by the listener, in their own words, that the parent log keeps. No coins, no timers, no rankings, no right-or-wrong answers: just slow, warm stories about kindness, patience and noticing small things, told at the pace the child sets.

## Features

- One stone, one complete story — three short parts with pauses to join in

## Sample content

### Sample · First Circle: Alex and the Stone That Remembered the Rain (complete story)

THE FIRST CIRCLE — Alex and the Stone That Remembered the Rain

The riverbank meadow was quiet in the way a held breath is quiet. Alex walked slowly between the grass and the water, looking — not for the biggest stone, not for the brightest, but for the one that felt like a story. There, half in the shallows, sat a smooth grey stone with a single white stripe running right around its middle, like a ribbon tied by the river itself.

"That one remembers the rain," said Mina the otter keeper, sliding onto the bank with water shining on her whiskers. She kept the stone circle for the river: she polished the stones, and she knew which were warm from the sun and which were cool from the deep. Tomas the stone collector nodded from the far side of the circle, his pockets gently clinking. "Grey with a white stripe," he said. "Rain stones are patient stones. Sit down, Alex. Hold it in both hands, and listen with your whole self."

Alex sat in the circle, and the stone grew warm in two small hands, and the story began.

PART ONE — THE POOL AFTER RAIN. Once, in this very meadow, there lived a small heron named Wren — no taller than a watering can, with legs like two kind twigs. Wren loved one thing best in all the world: her rain pool. After every shower it filled, clear and cool, at the edge of the reeds, and in it swam three silver fish who flashed like dropped spoons. But on the morning of our story the rain had been heavy, and the pool was all stirred up — brown and cloudy as cocoa. Wren could hear the little fish moving below the surface, but she could not see them at all.

[PAUSE — PREDICT] Here Mina stopped and smiled. "Alex, what do you think Wren did?" Alex thought about the cloudy water and said: "I think she dipped her beak in anyway, and splashed about, and got a beak full of mud." Mina laughed her otter laugh. "Hold that thought," she said. "The stone remembers what really happened."

PART TWO — WAITING LIKE A HERON. Wren very nearly did splash. She lifted one twig leg — and then she remembered what the old river teaches every heron: you cannot hurry water clear. So Wren did the hardest trick a heron knows. She stood. She stood on one leg in the soft mud, still as a fence post, still as a sleeping reed, while the clouds drifted away and the sun came out and laid a warm hand on the pool. Slowly — grain by grain, swirl by swirl — the mud settled. A water boatman skimmed past like a tiny rowing boat. A dragonfly stitched the air above the reeds. And the pool, little by little, turned from cocoa to tea, and from tea to glass.

[PAUSE — ADD A DETAIL] "Your turn to give the story something," said Tomas, his eyes twinkling. "One small detail — anything you like." Alex looked at the reeds and added: "There was a tiny green frog sitting on a leaf, watching too, and he waited just as still as Wren." "Then he is in the story now," said Tomas, "and the stone will keep him safe."

PART THREE — WHAT THE CLEAR WATER SHOWED. At last the pool was perfectly clear — and oh, it had been worth the wait. There were the three silver fish, flashing like dropped spoons. But there was something more: down at the bottom, in the soft silt, lay a small round stone, grey with a single white stripe, that the rain had washed in during the night. Wren fished it out gently, gently, and set it on the bank to dry in the sun — because even then, herons knew a rain stone when they saw one. "That," whispered Mina, "is the very stone in your hands. Wren waited for it, the way you waited to choose it."

Then it was time to return the stone to the river, the way every circle ends. Alex carried it to the shallows, knelt in the cool water, and said the retelling — one sentence, in Alex's own words: "A little heron named Wren stood perfectly still until the muddy pool turned clear, and her patience found her the fish — and this stone."

Alex let go. The stone settled onto the riverbed with the softest click, its white stripe shining up through the water like a smile. "Off it goes," said Mina, "to remember this story — and yours — until the next listener comes." And the river, very quietly, flowed on.
