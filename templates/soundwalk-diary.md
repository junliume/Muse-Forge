# The Soundwalk Diary

*Slow walks that teach you to listen to a place*

**Category:** Grown-ups · **Ages:** Adults · **Chinese name:** 声景漫步日记 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A gentle listening practice for grown-ups: take a short familiar walk, but attend only to sound. Each outing gives you a listening warm-up, three timed stops with a single focus at each stop, a hand-drawn sound map to sketch from memory, and a closing reflection page. No recording gear is needed — ears, a notebook, and unhurried attention are enough. Over weeks the diary becomes a portrait of a neighbourhood told entirely in what it sounds like, season by season.

## Features

- A listening warm-up that tunes attention in two minutes
- Three-stop walk structure — one sound focus per stop
- Sound-map sketching: draw what you heard, where it sat
- Closing reflection page with a single-sentence keepsake
- A seasonal index so winter and summer walks can be compared

## What's included

- Walk guide & listening warm-ups

- Sound-map sketch pages
- Reflection & seasonal index

## At a glance

- Two quiet minutes first — the warm-up tunes your ears before you take a single step
- Three stops, one focus each: water, then voices, then the silences in between
- Sketch a sound map from memory — near sounds large, far sounds small, silence left blank

## How it works

1. Start at your door with the two-minute listening warm-up — nearest sound, middle distance, farthest sound
2. Walk slowly through three timed stops, one sound focus at each: water, voices, then silence and pauses
3. Sketch a sound map from memory, close with a single-sentence keepsake, and file both in the seasonal index

## Sample content

### Sample Session · A Thirty-Minute Morning Loop: Fountain, Market Edge, Quiet Lane (complete worked session)

WARM-UP — TWO MINUTES STILL (do this at your front door or gate, before walking):
Stand still, feet grounded, notebook closed. Count ten slow breaths. On breaths 1–3, notice only the nearest sound you can hear — the one you could almost touch. On breaths 4–7, widen out: notice the middle distance — sounds across the street or garden. On breaths 8–10, listen for the farthest sound — a plane, a distant siren, wind in high branches. Write nothing yet. The point is to feel your hearing widen like a lens opening.

STOP ONE — THE FOUNTAIN (about 8 minutes, focus: WATER & WEATHER):
Walk at half your usual speed to the fountain. Stand where you can hear it clearly but it does not drown everything else. Listen for three layers and note one line for each:
Layer 1 — The main fall: is it steady or gusty? Does it patter, roar, or tick?
Example line: 'Main fall steady, a soft roar that fills the ears and makes nearby talk feel private.'
Layer 2 — The edges: drips, splashes, coins, a cup rinsing.
Example line: 'A single drip at the north edge, every 4 seconds, clear as a metronome.'
Layer 3 — What the water hides and reveals: when a gust thins the fall, what slips through — a bus brake, a child's laugh?
Example line: 'When the wind dropped, pigeon wings clapped twice behind the spray.'
Do not chase a 'beautiful' sound. An ugly, interesting sound written precisely is worth more than a pretty one written vaguely.

WALKING BETWEEN STOPS — THE THREAD:
Walk slowly. Pick ONE passing sound and follow it until it truly ends — a bicycle bell until its last shimmer, footsteps until they turn a corner. If it is cut off by a louder sound, note that too: 'Bell swallowed by delivery truck, 10:04.' These threads become the stitching of your sound map.

STOP TWO — THE MARKET EDGE (about 8 minutes, focus: HUMAN VOICES & BUSTLE):
Stand at the edge, not in the flow. Close your eyes for 30 seconds if it feels safe. Sort what you hear into three columns in your notebook: VOICES / HANDS & OBJECTS / MACHINES.
Example entries — Voices: 'Two vendors calling prices in turn, never overlapping — a polite duet.' 'A toddler narrating a croissant at full volume.' Hands & Objects: 'Crates stacked — wood on wood, three thuds, a pause, two more.' 'Paper bags shaken open like small sails.' Machines: 'Awning motor whirs, fridge hums a low B-flat all morning.'
Rule for voices: record rhythm, distance and feeling, never private words. You are sketching a soundscape, not transcribing people. If you can understand a private conversation, you are standing too close — move back.

STOP THREE — THE QUIET LANE (about 8 minutes, focus: SILENCE & PAUSES):
Choose the quietest stretch of your loop. This is the hardest stop. 'Silence' here never means no sound — it means the gaps become audible. Time one full minute with your notebook open and make a mark each time the sound fully pauses, even for a second. Example: 'Minute tally: 7 true pauses. Longest pause — 6 seconds after a crow called: leaf rustle, distant leaf-blower, then nothing, and in the nothing, my own sleeve brushing the notebook.'
Then listen for the smallest sounds the quiet reveals: Example lines: 'Sparrows in the hedge — not singing, just shifting, feather and twig.' 'A gate latch two houses down, oiled and careful.' 'My own breathing, slower than at Stop One.'

THE SOUND MAP — SKETCH FROM MEMORY (5 minutes, at home or on a bench):
Draw a loose oval for your loop — accuracy does not matter. Mark the three stops as dots. Now draw SOUNDS, not buildings: near sounds large and dark, far sounds small and pale, moving sounds as dotted threads along their path, pauses as clean blank patches you deliberately leave empty. Example legend from this session: 'Fountain = large soft circle, centre-left, with a thin drip-dot at its north edge. Market = tight cluster of small sharp marks at the oval's east rim. Quiet lane = the whole west side left mostly blank, with 7 tiny pause-ticks. Bicycle bell = dotted thread entering south, ending mid-map where the truck swallowed it.' Date the map. The same loop drawn in winter will look — and have sounded — entirely different.

CLOSING REFLECTION — THE SINGLE SENTENCE:
End every session with ONE sentence that could only be true of this place on this morning. Not a summary — a keepsake. Example: 'This morning my neighbourhood woke up in layers: water first, then hands, then voices, and underneath it all a quiet lane holding its breath seven times a minute.'
File the map and sentence in the seasonal index. After four walks, lay the maps side by side and write one paragraph: what is this place, when you only meet it by ear?
