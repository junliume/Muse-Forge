# The Sentence Workshop

*Build silly, strong sentences — grammar you can play with*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 6–9 · **Chinese name:** 句子工坊 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on grammar workshop where learners build sentences from word-blocks — who, did what, what, where, when — then stretch them with strong verbs and describing words, fix fragments and run-ons, and finish by writing a mini-paragraph. Every lesson shows one fully worked build first, then lets the learner build their own; answers and corrections are explained in plain words, never just marked wrong.

## Features

- Word-block sentence building — snap WHO + DID WHAT + WHAT + WHERE blocks into real sentences
- Strong-verb and describing-word upgrades that make plain sentences vivid
- Fragment vs complete-sentence sorting, every answer explained in plain words
- A mini-paragraph finale in every full session

1. Watch one fully worked build — WHO, DID WHAT, WHAT and WHERE blocks snapped into a complete sentence, explained block by block
2. Build your own sentence, then upgrade it with a strong verb and describing words
3. Sort fragments from complete sentences, then finish with a 3-sentence mini-paragraph

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · Benches 1–3: Build It, Upgrade It, Sort It (complete worked lesson)

BENCH 1 · THE WORKED BUILD — watch one sentence get built, block by block.
Every strong sentence is built from blocks, and each block has one job. Today's blocks sit on the bench in a row:
WHO block: "Mina the baker" — the who, the person doing the action.
DID WHAT block: "carried" — the action, what the who did.
WHAT block: "a tower of warm rolls" — the thing the action happened to.
WHERE block: "across the market square" — the place, where it all happened.
Snap the blocks together in order — WHO + DID WHAT + WHAT + WHERE — add a capital letter and a full stop, and the machine prints: "Mina the baker carried a tower of warm rolls across the market square."
Why is it complete? Check it like a builder: it has a who (Mina the baker) and a did-what (carried), and it makes complete sense all by itself — nobody is left asking "and then what?" A sentence with a who and a did-what that stands on its own is a complete sentence.

BENCH 2 · THE UPGRADE — swap one tired block for a stronger one.
"Carried" is a fine verb, but it is a tired one: it tells us the rolls moved, and nothing more. Swap in the strong verb "balanced" and the machine prints: "Mina the baker balanced a tower of warm rolls across the market square."
Why "balanced" is more vivid than "carried": it shows the tower wobbling high while Mina keeps it steady — you can almost see her careful steps.
Now slide in a describing word: "golden" goes right before "tower", so the WHAT block becomes "a golden tower of warm rolls", and the machine prints: "Mina the baker balanced a golden tower of warm rolls across the market square."
Why "golden" helps the reader picture it: it hands the reader a colour, so the tower stops being plain and starts to glow in your mind.

BENCH 3 · THE SORT — two trays: FRAGMENT and COMPLETE SENTENCE. A fragment is a piece of a sentence — something is missing, so it cannot stand alone. Sort these four, then check every answer and its reason:
1) "ran to the gate" → FRAGMENT. Reason: there is no who — we do not know who ran to the gate.
2) "The fox slept under the porch." → COMPLETE SENTENCE. Reason: it has a who (the fox) and a did-what (slept), and it makes complete sense alone.
3) "Because the rain stopped" → FRAGMENT. Reason: a joining word starts it, but the thought is unfinished — because the rain stopped… what happened next?
4) "Sam packed his bag and zipped it shut." → COMPLETE SENTENCE. Reason: it has a who (Sam) and two did-whats joined by "and" (packed, zipped), and it stands on its own.

YOUR TURN · THE MINI-PARAGRAPH FINALE — build a paragraph of exactly 3 sentences on your own bench:
Sentence 1: use the frame Who / Did what / Where — pick a who, a did-what and a where, and snap them together.
Sentence 2: take a plain verb and upgrade it to a strong verb that shows the action.
Sentence 3: join two ideas with "because" so the reader learns why.
One fully worked model paragraph, built by those three rules: "Nia the gardener carried a watering can across the sunny yard. She tipped the heavy can and drenched the thirsty tomato vines. She watered them early because the afternoon sun would dry the soil."
Now check the model against the rules: sentence 1 snaps together a who (Nia the gardener), a did-what (carried) and a where (across the sunny yard); sentence 2 upgrades the verb — "drenched" is a strong verb where a plain "poured" would only say the water moved; sentence 3 joins two ideas with "because". When your own paragraph passes all three checks, the workshop bell rings — lesson complete.
