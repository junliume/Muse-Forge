# The Keepsake Interview Studio

*Gentle interviews that turn conversations into keepsakes*

**Category:** Grown-ups · **Ages:** Adults · **Chinese name:** 留声访谈工作室 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A calm studio for interviewing someone you care about — a family elder, a friend, a neighbor, a mentor — and turning one unhurried conversation into a keepsake you can keep. Pick a theme and a tone, and the studio lays out the session for you: a warm opening that puts your guest at ease, three short chapters of story prompts with gentle follow-ups, and a closing ritual that ends with gratitude, not a quiz. No journalism training needed, no recording required — just good questions, real listening, and a written or audio keepsake guide at the end. All names are placeholders you replace with your own guest.

## Features

- A complete session arc: opening, three story chapters, closing ritual
- Gentle follow-ups that invite stories, never interrogation
- Written keepsake, audio keepsake guide, or both
- Consent and comfort check built into the opening — pause or skip any question, any time

## What's included

## Sample content

### Sample Session · Chapter One: The Kitchen Table Years (complete worked opening)

BEFORE YOU BEGIN (2 minutes): Choose a quiet, comfortable place. Offer water or tea. Say the comfort promise out loud: 'There are no right answers, we can skip anything, and we can stop whenever you like. I am here to listen.'

OPENING — ALEX (interviewer): 'Thank you for doing this, Sam. I have always loved your kitchen-table stories, and I would love to keep a few of them properly. May I take notes as we talk?'
SAM: 'Notes? It was just an ordinary kitchen.'
ALEX: 'That is exactly the kind I want. Ordinary, and yours.'

CHAPTER ONE — THE KITCHEN TABLE YEARS
Prompt 1: 'Picture the kitchen you ate in most as a child. What is the first thing you can see?'
Worked moment — SAM: 'A blue enamel pot. It had a chip on the rim, and everything tasted a little bit of that pot.'
Gentle follow-up (use the object, not a new topic): 'What lived in that pot most often? Who made it?'
SAM: 'Soup, mostly. My grandmother made it standing up, tasting from the spoon, never sitting until everyone else had a bowl.'
Follow-up (invite a person): 'What did she say while she cooked — or was she quiet?'
SAM: 'She hummed. When the humming stopped, the soup was ready. We all knew.'
Note for the interviewer: Write down the humming. Small sensory details like this are the gold of a keepsake — more than dates, more than summaries.

Prompt 2: 'Who sat where? Was there a seat that was somehow always one person's?'
Prompt 3: 'What was a celebration meal in that kitchen — and what made it different from an ordinary day?'
Prompt 4 (closing this chapter gently): 'If that kitchen table could keep one sound forever, what should it keep?'

CHAPTER BREAK — ALEX: 'That was lovely. Shall we pause, or shall I ask about the street outside that kitchen next? You choose.'

HOW A SESSION CONTINUES: Chapter Two follows the chosen theme (for example, The Street Outside — first friends, errands, and the walk to school), Chapter Three turns to a turning point chosen by the guest, never forced by the interviewer. Each chapter is 3–4 prompts, each prompt has one object follow-up and one person follow-up, and any prompt can be skipped with a simple 'let's pass on that one' — no explanation needed.

CLOSING RITUAL (last 5 minutes): ALEX: 'Of everything you told me today, is there one sentence you would want kept exactly as you said it?' Write that sentence word for word, read it back, and let Sam correct it. Then: 'Thank you. Next time, may I ask about...?' — and let Sam choose the next theme, or choose rest. The keepsake page for this session is: the verbatim sentence at the top, the blue enamel pot and the humming in the middle, and one line at the bottom — 'Told by Sam, listened to by Alex, with thanks.'
