# The Shadow Light Lab

*Hands-on light, shadow and measuring experiments for curious kids*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 6-10 · **Chinese name:** 影子光线实验室 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on science studio where a child becomes a light investigator: shine a torch, build a small tower, measure its shadow, change one thing at a time, and discover the rule for themselves. Every experiment follows the same honest science rhythm — Predict, Try, Measure, Explain — with real numbers to record, materials sorted as transparent, translucent or opaque, and a parent guide that shows the worked answers without giving them away first. No kit to buy: a torch, paper, a ruler and everyday objects are enough indoors, or sunlight works outdoors.

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · Morning Shadows: How Long Is a Shadow? (complete worked lesson)

THE BIG QUESTION: Stand outside on a sunny morning and your shadow stretches away from you like a long ribbon. Stand outside at midday and it shrinks to a puddle at your feet. The tower in this lesson never changes — so what does? Today Alex becomes a light investigator and finds the rule with a torch, a tower of blocks and a ruler. No guessing: predict first, then measure, then explain.

OBJECTIVE: Discover that a shadow gets longer when the light shining on an object is lower, and shorter when the light is higher — while the object itself stays exactly the same size.

MATERIALS: A torch (flashlight) with fresh batteries; a tower of blocks exactly 10 cm tall (build it, then measure it with the ruler to be sure — 10 cm, no more, no less); a ruler or tape measure; one large sheet of white paper laid flat on the table or floor as the ground sheet; a pencil; and the Shadow Record Chart from the lab pack. Everything comes from around the house — there is nothing to buy.

SAFETY NOTE, READ FIRST: Never look straight into a torch — it is far too bright for eyes. Never look at the sun, not even for a second. In this lab we only ever look at shadows, never at the light itself. If you take the lab outdoors, the sun does the shining and you watch the paper, not the sky.

STEP 1 — PREDICT: Alex builds the tower in the middle of the ground sheet and marks its base with a small pencil cross so it never moves. The torch will shine from one side only. Before switching anything on, Alex writes a prediction on the record chart: 'If I hold the torch lower, shining almost sideways, I think the shadow will get longer.' Why? 'Because low light has to stretch past the tower.' Prediction locked in — in this lab a prediction is a promise you test, not an answer you protect. A wrong prediction is not a mistake; it is the experiment working.

STEP 2 — TRY & MEASURE: Keep one rule for all three tries: the torch stays on the SAME side of the tower and the SAME distance away — 30 cm from the tower, measured with the ruler — every single time. Only the HEIGHT of the torch changes. That is fair testing: change one thing, keep everything else the same.
HIGH TORCH: Hold the torch high above the table, shining steeply down at the tower, 30 cm away. Alex marks where the shadow ends with a pencil dot and measures from the base of the tower to the dot: the shadow is 10 cm long.
MIDDLE TORCH: Lower the torch to about table height, still 30 cm away on the same side. Mark the new shadow's end and measure: the shadow is 17 cm long.
LOW TORCH: Crouch down and hold the torch low, shining almost sideways, still 30 cm away. Mark and measure: the shadow is 28 cm long.
Alex records all three tries in the record chart:
Torch position | Shadow length
High torch | 10 cm
Middle torch | 17 cm
Low torch | 28 cm

STEP 3 — EXPLAIN: Now the detective work. Did the tower grow? Alex measures it again: still exactly 10 cm tall. The tower never changed. The ground sheet never moved. The torch stayed 30 cm away every time. The ONLY thing that changed was the height of the light — and the shadow answered every time: high light, short shadow (10 cm); middle light, longer shadow (17 cm); low light, longest shadow (28 cm). Check the pattern in the numbers: 10 → 17 → 28. The shadow grows every time the light gets lower, and it never shrinks. That is the rule, discovered with real numbers: THE LOWER THE LIGHT, THE LONGER THE SHADOW.

WORKED ANSWERS (parent guide — let Alex answer first, then check together):
(1) Which torch position made the longest shadow? The low torch — 28 cm, nearly three times the tower's own height.
(2) Did the tower grow during the experiment? No. Alex measured it before and after: 10 cm both times. Only the light moved.
(3) Why are shadows long in the morning and evening but short at midday? Because the sun is low in the sky in the morning and evening — like the low torch — and high overhead at midday — like the high torch. Same sun, same you; only the height of the light changes, so the shadow changes.

PART 2 — THE SORTING GAME: Light does not treat every material the same way. Alex holds six everyday things, one at a time, between the torch and the ground sheet, and sorts each into one of three families:
• Clear plastic lid — TRANSPARENT. The light passes straight through and you can see clearly through it.
• White tissue paper — TRANSLUCENT. Light passes through, but it comes out blurred and fuzzy — you cannot see details through it.
• Wooden spoon — OPAQUE. It blocks the light completely and makes a proper shadow.
• Coloured sweet wrapper (clear coloured film) — TRANSPARENT. Light passes through; the colour tints the light, but you can still see through it.
• Frosted bathroom-style plastic cup — TRANSLUCENT. Light glows through it, but anything seen through it is blurred.
• Cardboard — OPAQUE. No light gets through at all; it makes the darkest shadow of the six.
Count check: 2 transparent, 2 translucent, 2 opaque — a perfect set. The words to keep: transparent means see-through, translucent means light gets through but blurred, opaque means the light stops here.

CLOSING CHALLENGE — TWICE AS LONG: The tower is 10 cm tall. Can Alex make a shadow exactly twice as long as the tower — 20 cm? The middle torch gave 17 cm and the low torch gave 28 cm, so the winning torch height hides somewhere between middle and low. Alex moves the torch down a little at a time from the middle height, measuring after every move — 17, 18, 19… — until the pencil dot lands as close to 20 cm as possible. Close counts: a shadow of 19, 20 or 21 cm wins the challenge, because real measuring is about getting close and knowing why, not about magic exact numbers.

PARENT NOTES: Let the child hold the ruler and make every measurement first — resist the urge to correct mid-measure. Accept close readings (a wobbly hand easily adds or loses a centimetre) and praise the careful recording, not just the result. The pattern in the chart matters far more than any single perfect number: if your child can point at 10 → 17 → 28 and say 'the light went down, so the shadow grew,' the science has landed. If one reading breaks the pattern, that is a gift, not a failure — measure again together and talk about what might have moved. Keep the worked answers in this guide until your investigator has explained the rule in their own words; then compare, and celebrate the match.
