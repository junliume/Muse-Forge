# The Umbrella Library

*Borrow an umbrella, hear the story it carried through the rain*

**Category:** Storytelling · **Ages:** Ages 4–9 · **Chinese name:** 雨伞图书馆 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A gentle storytelling library where each umbrella on the stand remembers one rainy-day journey and tells it as a short interactive story. The child picks an umbrella, hears where it has been, chooses what happens at the puddle, the bridge or the market stall, and returns it with a new ending the child invents. Rainy-day words — drizzle, puddle, awning, gutter — are met naturally inside the story, never quizzed, and every ending the child invents is kept in a log parents can reread together.

## Features

- Pick-an-umbrella story selection — choose a color from the stand and hear the journey that umbrella remembers
- Two gentle choices in every story — at the puddle, the bridge or the market stall, the child picks what happens next
- The child invents the ending — Muse retells it back exactly as told, so it becomes part of the umbrella's story
- Vocabulary in context — drizzle, puddle, awning and gutter met naturally inside the story, never quizzed
- Parent log of endings invented — every child-made ending is saved for families to reread together

## Sample content

### Sample Story · The Red Umbrella and the Market Puddle (complete told story)

THE UMBRELLA LIBRARY

Tucked into the warmest corner of the library, right beside the round window where the rain likes to tap, stands a wooden stand with twelve umbrellas. Red, teal, sunny yellow, plum — each one hangs from its own hook, and each one is dripping, very slowly, not rain but tiny golden sparkles. This is the Umbrella Library, and Keeper June will tell you the rule if you ask: "You don't borrow these umbrellas to stay dry. You borrow them to hear where they've been."

On the afternoon our story begins, Alex came in out of a drizzle with wet shoes and chose the red umbrella, the brightest on the stand. Keeper June lifted it down carefully, the way you lift a sleeping cat. "The red one?" she said. "Oh, it has been waiting to tell this one. It remembers a market day. Shall we listen?" Alex nodded and held the red umbrella open — indoors! Keeper June never minds — and the umbrella began to hum, very softly, the way umbrellas do when they remember. And this is the story it told.

THE RED UMBRELLA'S MEMORY

Once, on a grey and drizzly morning, the red umbrella was borrowed by Baker Milo, who had a basket of warm rolls to carry to the market and not a dry hand to spare. Off they went together: under the awning of the flower stall — drip, drip, drip went the awning's edge — past the fishmonger's silver buckets, along the gutter where the rain had made a tiny river that hurried along beside them, carrying one brave leaf like a boat. Baker Milo walked tall and dry beneath the red umbrella, and the rolls stayed warm, and everyone they passed said the same thing: "Morning, Milo! Fine umbrella!" — because a red umbrella on a grey day is a cheerful thing to see.

Then they came to the market puddle.

It was not an ordinary puddle. It was the BIG puddle, the famous one that spreads across the whole market lane whenever it rains, deep as a soup plate and wide as a table, and on the far side of it stood Baker Milo's stall, and the morning crowd, waiting for warm rolls. "Well, Red," said Baker Milo (bakers always talk to their umbrellas), "how shall we cross?"

And here, listener, the umbrella always pauses — because this is the part where YOU choose. There were three ways across the big puddle, and the umbrella remembers all of them. You might HOP it — one, two, three hops across the shallow edge, quick as a frog, if you don't mind wet toes. Or you might SAIL a leaf across it — set a leaf boat on the puddle with one roll aboard and let the tiny gutter-river carry it to the stall. Or you might BRIDGE it with a plank — and that is the way Baker Milo chose, so that is the way the umbrella tells it in full.

THE PLANK BRIDGE (told in full)

Baker Milo looked around the market lane, the way you should always look around before you invent something. Behind the vegetable stall he found a long, flat plank — a good, honest plank, dry on top, just wide enough for one careful foot at a time. He laid it across the big puddle, end to end, and pressed each end down with a stone so it would not wobble. Then he balanced the basket of rolls on his head — "steady, rolls" — held the red umbrella high like a flag, and walked across: heel, toe, heel, toe, slow as a tightrope walker, while the puddle below him reflected the whole grey sky and one very determined baker. Halfway across, the plank bowed — just a little, just enough to make the crowd gasp — but Baker Milo's feet were sure, and the red umbrella tipped this way and that way to keep the balance, and step by careful step he reached the far side dry-shod, the rolls still warm, the crowd applauding like thunder after rain.

And the leaf? The leaf boat set off anyway, all by itself, carrying one crumb that had tumbled from the basket — a crumb the size of a freckle — across the puddle in state, like a tiny queen on a royal barge. It arrived two minutes after Baker Milo, to great ceremony, and a sparrow ate the crumb for its breakfast, which everyone agreed was exactly right.

BACK AT THE LIBRARY

When the story ended, the red umbrella gave one last happy drip of sparkles onto the library carpet (Keeper June never minds that either), and Alex carried it back to its hook on the stand. "Every umbrella comes back with a new ending," said Keeper June, "because every listener leaves one." Then she opened the Endings Log — the big book where listeners' endings are kept — and asked the question she always asks: "What do YOU think happened next?"

ONE LISTENER'S ENDING (told in full, as an example)

Here is the ending one listener invented, written down exactly as it was told: "That evening, Baker Milo went back to the big puddle with a paintbrush and a pot of red paint. He painted a thin red line all along the plank — 'the umbrella line,' he called it — so that everyone who crossed after him would walk the exact brave path of the red umbrella. And the plank stayed there all winter, and on rainy mornings the whole market crossed the big puddle one by one, heel, toe, heel, toe, dry-shod and proud, and the red umbrella, hanging in the library, dripped one extra sparkle every single time."

Now it is your turn. Borrow the red umbrella — or the teal one, which remembers a ferry ride, or the yellow one, which once sheltered a brass band — and when you reach the big puddle, choose. Hop it. Sail the leaf. Bridge it. And when you bring the umbrella home to its hook, leave YOUR ending in the log, and Keeper June will read it aloud on the next rainy day.
