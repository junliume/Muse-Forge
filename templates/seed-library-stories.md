# The Seed Library

*Tiny seed packets that grow into gentle stories*

**Category:** Storytelling · **Ages:** Ages 4-9 · **Chinese name:** 种子图书馆 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A calm storytelling template where each visit the child chooses a seed packet from a little wooden library drawer — a Sunflower Seed, a Moonbean, a Raindrop Seed that is not a seed at all — and the seed grows in three gentle chapters: Planting, Sprouting, and Opening. The child decides what the plant needs, who visits it, and what it becomes, while Muse narrates slowly and checks the child's ideas back before continuing. No scores, no timers, no right answers — just growing a story at the pace of a real seed.

## Features

- A three-chapter growing arc — Planting, Sprouting, Opening — one sitting, or one chapter a night for three nights
- Child-led choices throughout: what the plant needs, who visits it, and what it becomes
- Slow, patient narration that checks the child's ideas back before the story grows on
- A parent story log keeps the child's own lines, chapter by chapter

## What's included

- Seed packet story engine (12 original seeds)
- Three-chapter growing arc
- Gentle choice moments — child leads
- Parent story log with the child's own lines

## At a glance

- Choose a packet from the seed library drawer — every seed grows a different kind of story
- Planting, Sprouting, Opening — three short chapters, one sitting or spread over three nights
- The child decides what the plant needs and who visits — Muse narrates and confirms each idea back

## How it works

1. Open the seed library drawer and choose one of the 12 seed packets
2. Grow the story through Planting, Sprouting and Opening, choosing what the plant needs at each stage
3. Close with the parent story log — the child's own lines, kept chapter by chapter

## Sample content

### Sample · The Moonbean That Grew at Night (complete three-chapter story)

CHAPTER ONE — PLANTING

The Seed Library was not a big library. It was a small wooden cabinet of drawers by the window, and every drawer held seed packets instead of books. On a quiet afternoon, Alex pulled the drawers open one by one. There was a Sunflower Seed, tall and golden on its packet. There was a Raindrop Seed, which is not a seed at all, but a story about rain that thinks it is one. And in the very last drawer, tucked right at the back, there was a small silver packet: a Moonbean — a silver bean that only grows at night.

Alex chose the Moonbean.

At home, Alex filled a blue pot with soft dark soil and set it on the windowsill, where the moon could see it. The bean went into the soil up to its middle, the way you tuck someone into bed. Then came the watering, and the watering had a rule, the way all good seed stories do: exactly three spoonfuls of water. One spoonful — sip. Two spoonfuls — sip, sip. Three spoonfuls — and that was all. Not four, because Moonbeans dream of moonlight, not puddles.

"Grow when you are ready," Alex whispered. "I will check on you tomorrow night."

CHAPTER TWO — SPROUTING

On the first night, nothing happened at all, which is exactly what seeds do first.

On the second night, Alex tiptoed to the windowsill in socks — and stopped. There, in the middle of the blue pot, stood a silver sprout, thin as a thread of moonlight. And it had two leaves. Not three, not one — two leaves, and each leaf was shaped like a tiny moon, curled at the edges, shining softly in the dark.

"You grew moons," Alex breathed. "Two little moons."

That was when the visitor arrived. A pale moth, no bigger than a thumbnail, fluttered against the window glass — tap, tap — asking to be let in to see the famous sprout. Alex thought about it carefully. The moth was gentle. The night was warm. And a plant with moon-shaped leaves probably likes visitors who fly by moonlight.

So Alex opened the window a crack — just a crack, wide enough for one moth and a little night air — and the moth sailed in and settled on the rim of the blue pot, fanning its wings slowly, like waving at an old friend.

CHAPTER THREE — OPENING

On the third night, Alex brought a cushion to the windowsill and sat very still, because the sprout looked different. Between its two moon leaves, a small bud had risen, round and silver and closed tight as a secret.

The moth came back through the crack in the window. The moon came up over the rooftops. And then — slowly, the way real flowers do it, petal by petal — the bud opened.

One small white flower. Only one, and it was enough. It glowed faintly — a soft silver-white light. The moth circled it once, twice, three times, delighted.

Alex leaned close. A flower that opens at night and glows in the dark is exactly the right place to keep a secret. So Alex whispered a secret to the flower — a small one, just for keeping — and the flower listened the way flowers do, with its whole face.

"I will keep it safe," its glow seemed to say, "until morning."

And it did. All night long the small white flower glowed quietly over its secret, the moth slept on the rim of the pot, and Alex slept too, dreaming of seeds.

In the morning the flower had closed, calm and silver, keeping its promise. And back in the Seed Library drawer, eleven more packets were waiting — a Sunflower Seed, a Raindrop Seed that is not a seed at all, and seeds nobody has chosen yet.

Which seed will you choose next?
