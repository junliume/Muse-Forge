# The Map Room: Geography Detectives

*Read maps, follow routes, and think like a cartographer*

**Category:** Education · **Ages:** Ages 7–11 · **Chinese name:** 地图工作室：地理小侦探 · **Spec version:** 1.0

## What it is

A hands-on geography studio where young explorers learn to read a map the way detectives read clues — compass directions, symbols and keys, scale and distance, grid coordinates, and landmark routes. Each session gives a real illustrated map to decode, questions whose answers are on the map, and a field journal page to draw their own version. No real places or personal locations — every town, park and island is invented.

## Features

- A real map to decode in every session — key, compass rose, scale bar and grid
- Five skills in rotation: compass, symbols, scale, grid coordinates, routes
- Worked answers that point back to the map evidence
- Field journal: redraw the map from memory, then check it
- Invented places only — no real addresses or locations

## What's included

- Map-reading studio
- Five-skill rotation
- Field journal pages
- Parent explorer report

## At a glance

- Decode the key — every symbol on the map is a clue with a meaning
- Follow the route string — north, east, and counting squares to the bridge
- Draw your own map in the field journal, then check it against the original

## How it works

1. Open the session map and learn its frame: grid, compass rose, key and scale bar
2. Answer detective questions whose answers are all on the map — then check the evidence
3. Close the map, redraw the town in the field journal from memory, and compare

## Sample content

### Sample Lesson · Lesson One: The Three Bridges Town (complete worked map lesson)

LESSON CARD — Explorer: Sam · Ages 7–8 · English · About 25 min · Town explorer · Curious detective · One-page map journal. Today's map is The Three Bridges Town, an invented town that exists only on this page. Every answer in this lesson is hiding somewhere on the map — your job is to catch it with evidence, the way a detective catches a clue.

MEET THE MAP
Spread the map flat and look at its frame first, the way a detective studies the edges of a photograph before the middle. The town is drawn on a grid of 5 columns and 5 rows — 25 squares in all. The columns run west to east, left to right across the page, and they are lettered A, B, C, D, E: column A is the westernmost edge of town and column E the easternmost. The rows run north to south, top to bottom, and they are numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, 5: row 1 is the northern edge of town and row 5 the southern edge. Every square has an address made of its column letter and its row number — letter first, number second — so B2 means column B, row 2. Say it the way a cartographer says it: 'B2.'

THE COMPASS ROSE
In the top corner of the map sits the compass rose, a star with four long points. The longest point aims at the top of the page and is marked N: north is at the top of this map, always. Turn the rose slowly in your mind — the bottom point is south, the right-hand point is east, the left-hand point is west. North up, south down, east right, west left. Every direction answer in this lesson is read off the rose; a geography detective never guesses a direction without checking it.

THE KEY — EVERY SYMBOL IS A CLUE
Down the side of the map is the key, the map's own dictionary, and it tells you what each little drawing means:
• An open book = the Library.
• A croissant = the Bakery.
• An arch = a Bridge.
• A tree = a Park.
• Three blue drops = a Fountain.
When you meet a symbol on the map, you never have to guess what it is — you look it up in the key, the way a detective checks a fingerprint against the file.

THE SCALE BAR — HOW BIG IS A SQUARE?
Under the key sits the scale bar, and it carries the most important number in the whole lesson: 1 grid square = 100 big steps. Walking one square — from one grid line to the next — is 100 big steps in the real town. Two squares is 200 big steps; three squares is 300. Cartographers use scale to turn 'a little way on paper' into 'how far your feet will actually go.'

THE FIVE PLACES — LEARN THE TOWN
Now walk the town with your finger and plant its five landmarks in your memory:
• The LIBRARY (open book) stands at B2 — column B, row 2, in the north-west of town.
• The BAKERY (croissant) stands at D2 — the very same row as the Library, two squares to its east.
• The CENTRAL FOUNTAIN (three blue drops) sparkles at C3 — the exact middle square of the whole map.
• OLD OAK PARK (the tree) spreads its shade at B4 — column B again, two squares south of the Library.
• The RIVER runs along row 5 at the bottom of the map, flowing across the whole southern edge — and three bridges cross it, at B5, C5 and D5.
Notice the pattern a cartographer would notice: the Library, Old Oak Park and the west bridge all share column B, like beads threaded on one string running north to south.

FIVE DETECTIVE QUESTIONS — EVERY ANSWER POINTS BACK TO THE MAP
Question 1 — KEY DETECTIVE. What is at C3?
Find square C3: column C, row 3. The symbol drawn there is three blue drops. Look it up in the key: three drops = a fountain. ANSWER: the Central Fountain. Evidence: the drops symbol at C3, matched against the key.

Question 2 — COMPASS DETECTIVE. The Bakery is at D2. In which direction is the Bakery from the Library at B2?
Both buildings stand in row 2, so the walk runs along a single line. From B2, travel along the row: C2, then D2. Moving towards column E means moving towards the right-hand point of the compass rose. ANSWER: the Bakery is EAST of the Library — the same row, two squares east. Evidence: same row number, letter two steps further east, and the rose says right is east.

Question 3 — SCALE DETECTIVE. How far is the walk from the Library at B2 south to Old Oak Park at B4?
Start at B2 and walk down column B: B3 is one square, B4 is two squares. The scale bar says 1 square = 100 big steps, so 2 squares = 2 × 100 = 200 big steps. ANSWER: 200 big steps. Evidence: two squares counted on column B, multiplied by the scale bar's 100.

Question 4 — ROUTE DETECTIVE. You are standing at the Central Fountain, C3. Which bridge is closest if you walk straight south?
Walk straight down column C: C4, then C5 — and C5 carries the arch symbol, which the key tells you is a bridge. The other two bridges, B5 and D5, would each need a sideways step first, so neither is on a straight-south walk. ANSWER: the bridge at C5. Evidence: the same column C, a straight line south, no turns at all.

Question 5 — THE ROUTE CHALLENGE. Start at the Bakery, D2. Walk west to the Fountain, then south to a bridge — and say every step out loud, square by square, the way a guide would.
Step 1: from D2, go WEST one square to C2.
Step 2: from C2, go SOUTH one square to C3. Arrived — the three blue drops of the Central Fountain, found by walking west, then south, from the Bakery.
Step 3: from C3, go SOUTH one square to C4.
Step 4: from C4, go SOUTH one square to C5 — the arch symbol, a bridge over the river.
The whole route in a cartographer's shorthand: D2 → C2 → C3 → C4 → C5. West one square, south three squares, and every square named.

FIELD JOURNAL — DRAW IT FROM MEMORY
Now close the map. On your field journal page, draw the empty grid first: five columns lettered A to E, five rows numbered 1 to 5. Sketch the compass rose in the corner, north at the top. Then place the five landmarks from memory — as symbols from the key, not words: the open book at B2, the croissant at D2, the three drops at C3, the tree at B4, and the river running along row 5 with its three arches at B5, C5 and D5. Write the scale bar underneath: 1 square = 100 big steps. Finally, open the real map and check your drawing square by square against the original. A landmark in the wrong square is not a failure — it is the exact square your memory will hold tightest next time. Detectives check their notebooks; cartographers check their maps. Today, you did both.
